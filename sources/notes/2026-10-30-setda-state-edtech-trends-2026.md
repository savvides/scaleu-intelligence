# State edtech offices want evidence they can check: SETDA's 2026 State EdTech Trends Report

- **Date observed:** 2026-10-30
- **Author:** Philippos Savvides
- **Type:** Research
- **Audience:** K–12
- **Evidence:** Survey of state education agency leaders (self-reported); state spotlights quoting agency staff (speaker claims); state laws checked at the statute
- **Source:** Evo Popoff and David Walker, [*2026 State EdTech Trends Report*](https://www.setda.org/priorities/state-trends/), produced by Whiteboard Advisors and commissioned by SETDA, released October 29, 2026 ([press release](https://www.setda.org/news/press-releases/press-releases-2026/new-report-ai-remains-states-priority-amid-growing-debate-about-technology-schools/)). The same page links the summary results and open-ended responses as spreadsheets. Page numbers below refer to the 28-page PDF.
- **Last reviewed:** 2026-10-30. Each figure below was checked against the report page cited. The Ohio, North Carolina, and Tennessee requirements were checked at the statute or the legislature's bill page. The secondary studies the report cites were not checked.

## The decision this informs

What a K-12 founder should have ready for state-level reviewers and purchasing cooperatives, and why vendor claims alone no longer carry a deal with them.

## What was observed

The survey drew 73 responses from state edtech directors, chief information officers, and other state agency leaders in 45 states and one territory (press release; p. 9). The report often writes "X% of states," but the figures are shares of respondents.

### Buying standards and vendor claims (survey)

- 53% said their state has published guidance on buying AI-enabled tools, up from about 40% last year (p. 22).
- Many of those guides use the [Five EdTech Quality Indicators](https://digitalpromise.org/initiative/edtech-quality-collaborative/) from the EdTech Quality Collaborative (1EdTech, CAST, CoSN, Digital Promise, ISTE, and SETDA): safe, evidence-based, inclusive, usable, and interoperable. Among states with AI procurement guidance, 2025 to 2026 (p. 22):

  | Indicator | 2025 | 2026 |
  |---|---|---|
  | Safe | 95% | 96% |
  | Evidence-based | 45% | 79% |
  | Inclusive | 50% | 75% |
  | Usable | 68% | 57% |
  | Interoperable | 59% | 39% |

- What carries the most weight when states buy: data privacy and security (82%), then evidence of effectiveness (50%). Accessibility, usability, and interoperability each drew 32% (p. 22).
- 68% agreed it is hard to independently verify vendor claims about AI products; none strongly disagreed. 32% said their state has clear, trusted sources of information (pp. 22–23).
- Sources states rely on most for edtech and AI decisions: national organizations such as SETDA and CCSSO (62%), peer states (55%), independent research or evaluation organizations (48%), district data (38%), vendor documentation or demos (28%), federal guidance (28%) (p. 23).
- For future AI guidance, respondents named bias, safety, and student protections (92%), evidence standards for AI-enabled products (73%), data governance and model-training disclosures (71%), and vendor transparency (56%) (p. 12).

### Data on use and effectiveness (survey)

- 52% said their state collects no data on the use or effectiveness of edtech tools, 26% collect some data on one of the two, and 22% on both (p. 17).
- 67% chose "We have a lot of edtech but don't always use it effectively," up from 56% in 2025. 5% said their state needs more edtech (p. 16).
- 69% said a request or requirement from the governor or legislature would make them more likely to collect effectiveness data (p. 17).

### AI moving from guidance to law

- 23% said their state's AI guidance is now required by state law, up from 4% in 2024 (p. 11).
- **Ohio.** [ORC 3301.24](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3301.24), enacted in House Bill 96 and effective September 30, 2025, requires each school district, community school, and STEM school to adopt an AI-use policy by July 1, 2026. Schools may adopt the department's model policy.
- **North Carolina.** [Session Law 2026-41](https://www.ncleg.gov/EnactedLegislation/SessionLaws/PDF/2025-2026/SL2026-41.pdf), section 7.39 (Senate Bill 257, approved July 7, 2026), contains these provisions:
  - Public school units must adopt AI-use policies by June 30, 2027, after reviewing a model policy that DPI must publish by December 31, 2026.
  - The State Board must add AI literacy standards to the computer science course of study for the 2028–29 school year.
  - Teachers must complete Friday Institute AI training by June 30, 2028.
  - New G.S. 115C-102.14 directs DPI to keep an evaluation framework for generative AI tools covering privacy, alignment with standards, and accessibility. DPI must publish a list of reviewed tools, set procurement guidance and qualified vendor lists that favor reviewed tools, and publish a list of all AI tools used in public school units.
- **Tennessee.** [Public Chapter 1056](https://wapp.capitol.tn.gov/apps/BillInfo/Default.aspx?BillNumber=HB0545&GA=114) (SB 677 / HB 545, signed May 27, 2026) requires teachers in grades 6–12 to complete a department-approved AI course by August 1, 2028, or within two years of licensure, whichever is later. The department must offer a free course.

### State spotlights (agency staff, as reported)

- **Illinois (pp. 23–25).** The Learning Technology Center of Illinois runs a free statewide purchasing cooperative. Through the Association of Educational Purchasing Agencies (AEPA), it is helping build a national bid for AI tools. Staff said:
  - Traditional procurement looks for three to five years of financial history, which few new AI companies have, and the AI bid aims to find vendors likely to last and to protect districts from steep renewal increases.
  - Contracts are reviewed for accessibility and Universal Design for Learning, and bid reviewers check whether a vendor offers a full range of accessible products.
  - Because districts braid funds from several budgets, curriculum coordinators, business officials, and superintendents now sign off on more orders, not only technology directors.
  - Districts sometimes ask questions vendor sales teams can't answer, and vendors sometimes say they meet the quality indicators with no way for buyers to verify it.
- **North Carolina (pp. 17–19).** NCDPI plans a platform, from August 2027, that tracks how often specific edtech apps are used; a second phase would link usage to assessment results. The state plan's goal is to renegotiate or end contracts that fall below return-on-investment benchmarks. NCDPI also provides a data sharing agreement districts can use with vendors; its CIO said most security incidents in the state's schools originate with third parties.
- **Tennessee (pp. 13–14).** The department's legal team is writing a checklist for districts to use before signing AI vendor contracts, and to revisit those contracts every year.

### Other context (survey)

- Professional learning was named a top unmet technology need by 62%, up from 47%; funding remains first at 72% (p. 26).
- 76% of states have five or fewer staff working on edtech; 27% have one person (p. 27).
- 57% reported a strict statewide ban on student cell phones, up from 22% in 2025 (pp. 20–21). The report cites other studies on phone bans; read those at their sources.

## What it may mean

ScaleU interpretation, not the report's:

- **For K-12 founders, "evidence-based" is becoming a written criterion in state guidance.** The evidence-based indicator nearly doubled as a procurement indicator. North Carolina now has a statute requiring an AI-tool review framework and a public list of reviewed tools. Prepare documentation a reviewer can check without trusting your word: ESSA tier, study design, privacy terms, accessibility conformance. Vendor documentation ranked near the bottom of trusted sources.
- **Usage data will be compared with outcomes.** Most states can't do this yet. Some are starting, and North Carolina ties it to contract renewal. A founder who can report outcomes, not logins, is ahead of where many states are.
- **Cooperatives and multi-state bids can open or close a market.** A national AI bid that screens for financial longevity may disadvantage young companies; find out how to qualify early.
- **AI-use policies at the district level give buyers a list of questions to ask you.** Ohio's are already due; North Carolina's are due June 30, 2027.

## Limits and open questions

- Respondents are state agency officials, not the district staff who sign most contracts. State guidance shapes district buying, but this survey doesn't measure how much.
- With 73 responses, a single response moves a percentage by more than a point. Year-to-year changes include changes in who answered.
- Whiteboard Advisors also produced the Beyond the Ban analysis the report cites on phone policies.
- The spotlights describe plans (North Carolina's 2027 platform, the AEPA AI bid) that may change.
