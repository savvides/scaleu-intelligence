# Sole-source justification

A draft your champion can adapt when asking the procurement office to buy without a competitive bid. A good justification states the requirement first, shows which alternatives were checked and why each falls short, and makes only claims the champion can defend in an audit.

**Applies to:** public K-12 districts and public or private colleges in the United States.

Rules differ by state, by institution, and by funding source, and the buyer's procurement office decides whether a purchase qualifies. Most institutions have their own form; this template supplies the content for it. It is a ScaleU template, not legal advice. If another product can meet the requirement, the purchase is not sole source and the honest route is quotes or a bid.

## What the rules look like

Four public examples, to show the range. Read your buyer's own policy before drafting.

| Source | What it says |
|---|---|
| Federal funds: [2 CFR 200.320(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.320) | A recipient of a federal award may buy noncompetitively only in five circumstances: the purchase is within the micro-purchase threshold; it can only be fulfilled by a single source; a public exigency or emergency will not permit the delay of competition; the federal agency or pass-through entity approves a written request; or competition is determined inadequate after soliciting several sources. The thresholds are defined by reference to the Federal Acquisition Regulation ([2 CFR 200.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.1)). This governs purchases paid from federal awards. |
| A university: [University of Arizona, policy 4.7](https://policy.arizona.edu/procurement-policy/ppm-47-sole-source) | Sole source means only one source of supply exists, and is the exception. A department's preference for a vendor after quotes or demos does not qualify, and neither do business purpose or budget. The chief procurement officer or a designee approves the justification form. |
| A university: [Michigan State University](https://procurement.msu.edu/procurement/policies-procedures/single-sole-source) | Distinguishes sole source (no alternative exists) from single source (alternatives exist, but only one meets the need). Purchases above $25,000 need a justification form to skip bidding, the justification must be defensible in an audit, and the procurement office makes the final determination. |
| A state K-12 statute: [Texas Education Code 44.031](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._educ._code_section_44.031) | District contracts of $50,000 or more in a 12-month period must use a listed competitive method. Subsection (j) exempts an item available from only one source, including one for which a patent, copyright, secret process, or monopoly precludes competition. |

Two lessons hold across them. The test is whether anyone else can meet the requirement, not whether your product is better. And the institution, not the vendor, makes and documents the finding.

## Template

**Sole-source justification**
Requesting department: [ ]. Requester: [champion, title]. Date: [ ].
Vendor: [Company legal name]. Product: [ ]. Amount: $[ ] for [term]. Funding source: [budget line; state whether federal funds are used].

**1. Requirement.** [Department] needs to [function] for [population] by [date], in order to [institutional goal or mandate]. Stated as a need, without naming the product.

**2. Essential features.** The requirement cannot be met without:

| Essential feature | Why it is essential to the requirement |
|---|---|
| [For example, integrates with the institution's existing system through a named standard] | [ ] |
| [Content, data, or method available only under the vendor's patent, copyright, or license] | [ ] |
| [Continuity with an evaluated pilot whose data and configuration carry over] | [ ] |

**3. Market search.** On [dates], the department searched for alternatives by [method: peer institutions asked, cooperative contracts and existing campus licenses checked, vendors contacted].

| Alternative considered | Essential feature it lacks | How we know |
|---|---|---|
| [Product or approach A] | [ ] | [vendor documentation / demo on date / written reply] |
| [Product or approach B] | [ ] | [ ] |
| [Existing campus tool or in-house build] | [ ] | [ ] |

**4. Sole-source basis.** [Company] is the only source because [it holds the patent, copyright, or exclusive license to feature / it is the sole developer and does not sell through resellers / no other product provides the essential features in section 2]. Vendor letter confirming this is attached.

**5. Price reasonableness.** The price is fair and reasonable because [it matches the vendor's published price list / it equals or betters the price paid by named comparable institutions / it compares with the cost of the nearest alternatives as follows].

**6. Federal funds.** [Not applicable / This purchase uses federal award number, and qualifies under 2 CFR 200.320(c) because …]

**7. Certification.** I have no financial interest in the vendor. The statements above are accurate to the best of my knowledge. [Signature, title, date.]

**Procurement office determination.** [Approved / denied], by [name], [date].

## What you supply to the champion

- A signed letter on company letterhead stating what is exclusive to you: patents, copyrights, sole distribution. State only what is true; the letter goes in a public file.
- Your price list, or a statement that the quoted price is no higher than comparable customers pay.
- The feature comparison behind section 3, with sources, for the champion to verify and rewrite.
- Any existing contract the buyer could use instead: a state contract, a purchasing cooperative, or a consortium agreement may be faster than a sole-source request.

## Common mistakes

- Arguing that the product is best. Preference is the reason these policies give for denial.
- Skipping the market search. A justification with no alternatives listed reads as no search done.
- Leaning on the pilot alone. Prior use supports continuity, and procurement offices rarely accept it as the whole case.
- Splitting an order to stay under a threshold. If the buyer's policy prohibits it, your champion carries the risk.
- Writing it as the vendor. The requester certifies it, so every sentence must be theirs.
- Treating approval as certain. Keep a second route open: an existing contract, quotes, or a smaller first purchase under the threshold (see the [procurement guide](../selling/procurement-guide.md)).
