# Operating principles

Seven beliefs shape every guide in this library and every company that goes through ASU ScaleU. Read them to understand the stance behind the advice; follow each link for the method.

**Applies to:** all segments. The principles draw mainly on higher-ed work; where one is specific to K-12 or higher ed, it says so. They are practitioner judgment, not research findings.

Informed by [Cracking Higher Ed: Why Startups Miss the Mark](../sources/events/2026-sxsw-edu/README.md) (SXSW EDU 2026, CC BY 4.0).

## 1. Validate demand, not interest

Interest costs a buyer nothing. A waitlist is interest. A dean who calls the product transformative is interest. Demand is a budget holder who can name the line item, a procurement process that starts before the pilot ends, and a product that survives the departure of its champion.

Before you build, answer five questions. Who is struggling, and at what moment? What have they already tried? Is there a budget line item? What happens if they do nothing? Who else must say yes? A founder who cannot answer all five with specifics is not ready to pilot. The [demand validation test](demand/demand-validation.md) gives each question its probes and a scoring table.

## 2. Build upstream of the symptom

A problem surfaces in one phase of the student journey and begins one or two phases earlier. Students drop a course in week two, and founders build retention dashboards. The failure came months before, when nobody translated "7.5-week accelerated session" into "15 hours a week on top of your job."

When you hear a problem, ask what happened one phase earlier that made it inevitable. The upstream job usually has fewer competitors and more effect. See [the upstream-cause pattern in founder traps](demand/founder-traps.md#pattern-2-the-upstream-cause--downstream-symptom-split).

## 3. Evidence over enthusiasm

Most founders overrate their evidence. A teacher who says her students loved it has given a testimonial. High usage shows engagement. A gain from pretest to posttest without a comparison group shows that time passed.

If you sell to K-12, know where you stand on the [ESSA evidence tiers](evidence/evidence-tiers.md) and which tier your buyer requires. Higher-ed and corporate buyers do not use the tiers, but they ask the same thing: what changed, and how do you know? Have a plan to close the gap; the [evidence roadmap](evidence/evidence-roadmap.md) lays one out by company stage.

## 4. The buyer is not the user

Students describe teaching themselves. Faculty describe class sizes they cannot sustain. Institutions file these as separate problems in separate reports. They are one constraint seen from two positions.

The tool that relieves the provider's capacity often improves the student's experience as a byproduct, and the provider's side is where the budget and the purchasing authority sit. When you hear a student problem, find its mirror on the provider side. See [the two-jobs pattern in founder traps](demand/founder-traps.md#pattern-4-same-problem-two-jobs) and the [buyer personas](demand/buyer-personas.md).

## 5. Check the data before you trust the story

Every stakeholder may name the same problem, and the outcome data may still say otherwise. Interviews overrepresent the people who struggled. The majority who succeeded quietly leave no trace in them.

Treat unanimous agreement as a reason to count, not a license to build. If the outcome data contradicts the story, the opportunity may lie somewhere other than where everyone points. See [the stakeholder pattern in founder traps](demand/founder-traps.md#pattern-3-when-stakeholders-lie-unintentionally).

## 6. Narrow beats wide

"We sell to universities" names no market. Name one person at one institution with one problem. The smallest product someone will pay for this term is worth more than the platform you plan.

Your first ten customers should look alike: the same kind of institution, the same buyer, the same problem. Prove the product there, then expand from strength. The [go-to-market guide](selling/go-to-market.md) shows how to choose that first segment and motion.

## 7. A crowd is a warning

In higher ed, most founders build for the course experience, the most crowded phase of the student journey. The SXSW EDU 2026 talk put the share at about 80% of founders across more than 15 product categories; that figure is ScaleU's estimate, not a published count. The phases before a student reaches a classroom (pre-enrollment, application, onboarding, course selection) carry measured pain and few products.

A crowd of founders in one space proves only that the problem is easy to see. The [higher-ed jobs atlas](demand/higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md) maps the phases with fewer builders.
