# AI risk and trust for edtech founders

Use this before you ship a model that students talk to, or when a buyer asks what your AI does to learning. It answers one founder question: what can AI do to learners, and to the trust between learners and the people around them, and what design choices reduce that risk?

**Audience.** Founders selling to K–12 districts, colleges and universities, and corporate L&D. Read the evidence with its population in mind. Most of the developmental and anthropomorphism material below concerns children and adolescents. The Gallup poll covers ages 14 to 29, the closest evidence here for a college or workforce product.

**Evidence type.** Most of this page reports what speakers said at the ASU+GSV 2026 summit (April 12–15, 2026). Those are speaker claims, not verified results. Where a speaker cited academic research, this page sets the claim beside what the research itself reports, checked against the papers on 2026-10-30. The Gallup survey has been checked against Gallup's published results. The summit transcripts are machine-generated and carry no speaker labels. Names follow each session's speaker list, the summit report, and context; where context leaves the speaker unclear, this page credits the session. As of the summit, the cognition question was the most contested theme of the week, and platform companies and practitioners were not in the same room on it.

## The honest caveat, up front

The most-cited number at the summit cuts against the hype. Ben Riley (Cognitive Resonance) cited Stanford SCALE's review of 800 studies of LLMs in education and said only 20 showed causal impact, and virtually none of those were positive ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/64-does-ai-expand-human-intelligence-or-atrophy-it.md)). The review itself bears out the count but not the direction: Lily Fesler, JP Martinez Claeys, Chris Agnew and Susanna Loeb, [*The Evidence Base on AI in K-12: A 2026 Review*](https://scale.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/The%20Evidence%20Base%20on%20AI%20in%20K-12%20Report.pdf) (AI Hub for Education, Stanford SCALE Initiative, 2026). The authors screened the more than 800 papers in their repository as of October 2025. Those papers cover AI in K–12 education, not only LLMs, and are mostly preprints. The authors found 20 with strong causal evidence. They found no high-quality causal studies of US K–12 students; much of the work studies people over 18, outside the US, in short sessions. The authors report these findings:

- AI tools improved students' performance on math practice, programming and writing while students had access to them.
- When students were assessed without AI, the effects were mixed.
- Making work easier can come at the expense of deeper thinking.
- Tools with pedagogical guardrails showed more promise than general-purpose tools.
- Teachers who used AI for lesson planning spent less time without losing quality.
- Regular automated AI feedback to human tutors improved instructional quality and student outcomes, especially for less experienced and lower-rated tutors.

They call these findings preliminary.

The count holds, and the causal research base is thin. Almost everything else below is panel assertion: operators reporting what they see, not controlled trials. Treat it as signal worth designing around, not proof.

Riley's framing is the one to hold even if you reject his conclusion. He said a tool was already being used by roughly 500 million students, by OpenAI's own account (his figure, not checked here), before the longitudinal data exists. Wait for the RCTs to settle and you decide with a five-year lag. Deploy now without measurement and you become the data. Pick your error on purpose.

## The cognition and trust risks

### Cognitive automation, not offloading (speaker claim)
Riley named the behavior "cognitive automation," a term he credited to François Chollet, rather than the gentler "cognitive offloading." He described Carnegie Mellon and UCLA work in which students who had grown used to AI gave up sooner when they later tried to learn without it; he said the researchers call this "cognitive surrender" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/64-does-ai-expand-human-intelligence-or-atrophy-it.md)).

The study that matches his description is Grace Liu, Brian Christian, Tsvetomira Dumbalska, Michiel Bakker and Rachit Dubey, ["AI Assistance Reduces Persistence and Hurts Independent Performance"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04721) (arXiv preprint, 2026, not peer reviewed). Its first and last authors are at Carnegie Mellon and UCLA. Its three randomized experiments enrolled 1,222 US adults recruited online, not students in a course. Each experiment was a single online session of about 13 to 15 minutes, in which participants worked on fraction problems or SAT-style reading questions with or without an AI assistant. Afterward, working without AI, the AI groups solved fewer problems than the control groups in all three experiments. They skipped more problems in two of the three; in the second experiment the difference was not statistically significant.

That paper doesn't use the term "cognitive surrender." The term appears in a different preprint, by Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave of the Wharton School: [*Thinking—Fast, Slow, and Artificial: How AI is Reshaping Human Reasoning and the Rise of Cognitive Surrender*](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=6097646) (SSRN, 2026). They use it to mean adopting an AI's output with minimal scrutiny. In their three preregistered experiments (N = 1,372), accuracy on reasoning problems rose when the AI was right and fell when it was wrong.

On a different panel, a speaker said Sal Khan's chief learning officer had reported students typing "IDK" into Khanmigo rather than engaging with it ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/80-is-ai-amplifying-cognition-or-dismantling-it.md)). The worry the speakers raised isn't only that students think less while using AI. It's that the capacity itself erodes.

### Kids don't want the chatbot tutor (operator signal)
Dan Meyer (Amplify) watches one benchmark: whether kids actually want to talk to a chatbot tutor. He said it has sat flat at roughly 5% for three years. His build rule follows from it: "not AI direct to student," but AI through the teacher, as an analytical layer. Joe Davis (KAIT Lab) is working toward the same goal with AI-powered infrared pens that surface where students get stuck inside a problem set, so productive struggle stays the load-bearing part. — Dan Meyer (Amplify), Joe Davis (KAIT Lab) ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/80-is-ai-amplifying-cognition-or-dismantling-it.md))

### Killing the butterfly (operator signal)
Larry Berger (Amplify) gave the most evocative warning. AI has remarkable capabilities, he said, but the implementations he sees are "killing the butterfly": the moment of collective wonder that pollinates the next thousand moments of learning. His board gave him six months to largely step back from running the company and work out whether AI can keep the butterfly alive. A couple of months in, he said he had no answers yet. That matters: a serious operator with every incentive to be bullish is openly unsure. — Larry Berger (Amplify) ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/63-learning-amplified-with-larry-berger-and-russlynn-ali.md))

### Sycophancy and the praise problem (speaker claim)
Isabelle Hau (Stanford Accelerator for Learning) shared a stat from one of her visiting scholars: AI models praise children 13 times more often than humans do. She didn't name the study, and no published source for the figure was found as of 2026-10-30. The closest published work concerns adults, not children: across 11 AI models, Myra Cheng and colleagues found that the models affirmed users' actions 49% more often than humans did (["Sycophantic AI decreases prosocial intentions and promotes dependence"](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352), *Science*, 2026). It is not the source of Hau's figure. Her read as a parent: if a model praises my child 13 times more than I do, kids start to expect it, and human-to-human relationships shift to match. The structural problem underneath: companies are incentivized to optimize for engagement, and sycophancy is a reliable way to get it. Your retention metric and the learner's development can point in opposite directions. — Isabelle Hau (Stanford Accelerator for Learning) ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/48-every-student-should-have-an-ai-learning-companion.md))

### Anthropomorphism and developmental risk (operator signal)
Scale makes this urgent. Prateek Maheshwari (Physics Wallah) described "mega classrooms" where 100,000 students learn in one live, teacher-led session, with AI handling students' questions, at a price of about $40 for a year. The student feedback he hears keeps returning to one line: "AI is not judging us" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/49-the-billion-student-ambition-scaling-education-in-the-age-of-choice.md)). That's the appeal and the hazard in one sentence. On the anthropomorphism panel, Tanya Gamby (Southern New Hampshire University) pointed out that real relationships depend on "rupture and repair." Matthew Biel (Georgetown) described interactions with AI as "not mutual" but "transactional," and warned that adolescents learn relationships through exactly that friction, which a model that never judges and never pushes back cannot provide. The panel's moderator (the summit report names Paul LeBlanc) was blunter: "AI is going to make social media look like a day at the beach." — Prateek Maheshwari (Physics Wallah), Tanya Gamby (SNHU), Matthew Biel (Georgetown) ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/84-living-learning-with-aliens-the-complex-psychology-of-ai-anthropomorphism.md))

### Tools built for adults, handed to kids (operator signal)
Lovable builds software for "the 99%" of people who aren't developers. Introducing Anton Osika at the summit, the moderator said Lovable had "allegedly" reached $400M in annual recurring revenue within two years. The company later confirmed it crossed $400M in February 2026 ([TechCrunch, March 11, 2026](https://techcrunch.com/2026/03/11/lovable-says-it-added-100m-in-revenue-last-month-alone-with-just-146-employees/)). In the same session, a speaker described nine-year-old entrepreneurs selling real products from sites they built with it. The underlying tools were not built for kids. Imagi partners with Lovable to bring it into K–12 classrooms as the layer for younger users. If your product reaches minors, directly or because a teacher points it at a class, assume the base model was tuned for adults. The age-appropriateness layer is yours to build, not something you inherit. Expect district-grade versions of that layer to become a product category, not a feature. — Lovable and imagi fireside chat ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/94-fireside-chat-with-lovable-and-imagi.md))

### Falling hope, rising anger (survey finding)
Kevin Roose (NYT) and Casey Newton (Platformer) brought up a Gallup survey, run with the Walton Family Foundation and GSV Ventures, of 1,572 people aged 14 to 29, fielded February 24 to March 4, 2026. [Gallup reports](https://news.gallup.com/poll/708224/gen-adoption-steady-skepticism-climbs.aspx) that the share who feel hopeful about AI fell nine points in a year, to 18%. Excitement fell 14 points, to 22%. The share who feel angry rose nine points, to 31%, and anxiety held at 42%. Usage held steady, with 51% using generative AI at least weekly. In the same session, Garrett Lord (Handshake) warned of an "agency divide" between people who manage AI and people AI manages ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/71-is-ai-eating-education-interview-series-with-kevin-roose-casey-newton.md)). The learners you serve are not uniformly excited. A meaningful share are anxious or angry, and they can tell which side of that divide a product puts them on.

### The other side of the table (the optimist case, for honesty)
The bull case came from James Donovan, head of learning and cognitive outcomes at OpenAI. His argument: the question isn't whether AI helps cognition but how the model is tuned. Model behavior elicits a human behavior, that behavior ladders up to cognitive outcomes over time, and tuned toward pedagogical alignment you get metacognitive gains. He pointed to a 20,000-student research deployment in Estonia as the model for generating real evidence ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/64-does-ai-expand-human-intelligence-or-atrophy-it.md)). OpenAI's [announcement](https://openai.com/index/the-next-phase-of-education-for-countries/) (May 20, 2026) describes a nationwide ChatGPT Edu deployment that Estonia's Ministry of Education leads through the AI Leap Foundation, reaching more than 20,000 students and 4,600 teachers. It says OpenAI is working with AI Leap, the University of Tartu and Stanford to study how AI affects those students' learning, and has committed to publishing the findings. As of 2026-10-30, no study design or results had been published. Note what this is and isn't: a stated thesis plus a study still running, not a result. Omar Abbosh (Pearson) summed up both camps: "If you use it wrong, you will absolutely get dumber. If you use it right, you can get smarter." The institution's job, and your product's job, is enforcing the difference. — James Donovan (OpenAI), Omar Abbosh (Pearson) ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/71-is-ai-eating-education-interview-series-with-kevin-roose-casey-newton.md))

## The design responses founders can adopt

These came up at the summit as concrete moves, not theory. Each is a panel assertion about what's being built, not a proven outcome.

### Friction and limits by design
Ann Wang described OMA Play, a screenless audio device for children aged three to five that is still in early research and development. It has no face. It takes naps during the day, shuts off at night, and parents control when it engages. Wang said it "might interact with you" about 60% of the time and will never give an answer right away ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/84-living-learning-with-aliens-the-complex-psychology-of-ai-anthropomorphism.md)). The principle generalizes past toddlers: build friction in deliberately. Time limits are a design lever, not a parental-controls afterthought. For a student-facing tutor, the limit is part of the pedagogy. It protects productive struggle and shows a buyer that you are not optimizing a learner's screen time to the ceiling. The sycophancy problem forces the same product question on every tutoring builder: what friction do you build in on purpose, and how do you measure when praise or agreement is hurting the learner instead of just retaining them?

### Keep the human as the user, when the evidence is thin
Meyer's "AI through the teacher" is a posture, not a constraint you're stuck with. Until your own outcome data says otherwise, aiming the model at the teacher or instructor, where it surfaces who's stuck, drafts feedback and flags patterns, carries less developmental risk than aiming it at the learner. ScaleU's practitioner read, not a measured result, is that a teacher-facing design also clears an institution's AI review faster.

### The institutional default
ScaleU's [summit report](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/synthesis/summit-intelligence-report.md#theme-7-ais-effect-on-cognition) proposes this default for buyers; it is ScaleU's synthesis of the cognition sessions, not a speaker's claim. You can adopt it as a product stance: assume model defaults push toward cognitive automation, not learning. Demand productive friction in the student-facing layer. Invest in teacher capability, not chatbot seats. Build the tool that makes the institution's enforcement job easier, not the one that quietly does the thinking for the student.

## What institutional buyers will ask

The questions above are about learners. Buyers will also review your AI as a vendor risk. This page doesn't repeat that material; each item links to the guide that covers it.

- **Student data.** Who sees it, where it goes, and whether it trains a model. Federal and state privacy law (FERPA, COPPA, state student-privacy laws) is in [K–12 regulation](../market/k12-regulatory.md). FERPA covers colleges too: once a student turns 18 or enters a postsecondary institution at any age, the rights [transfer from the parents to the student](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/what-ferpa). Colleges often send EDUCAUSE's [HECVAT](https://www.educause.edu/hecvat) security questionnaire. See [procurement](../selling/procurement-guide.md) for where the IT and privacy review sits in a deal.
- **Security.** Whether the model can be tricked into leaking records. See the lethal trifecta in the cross-links below.
- **Evidence of learning impact.** What your outcome data shows, and how strong it is. See [evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md).
- **Fit with institutional policy.** Colleges are writing AI-use and academic-integrity policies. Accessibility review and VPATs apply to AI features too. See the [higher-ed landscape](../market/highered-landscape.md).
- **Funding.** On July 22, 2025, the U.S. Department of Education [told grantees](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-issues-guidance-artificial-intelligence-use-schools-proposes-additional-supplemental-priority) that federal grant funds may support AI-enhanced high-impact tutoring, AI-based instructional materials, and AI for college and career advising, provided the use meets each program's rules. The guidance also set principles for responsible use, including attention to privacy and to engaging parents and other stakeholders.

## Cross-links

- The trust risk has a security twin. Simon Willison calls it the ["lethal trifecta"](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/16/the-lethal-trifecta/): a model with access to private data, exposure to untrusted content, and the ability to communicate externally can be tricked into leaking that data. A student-facing model that touches FERPA records, reads student-supplied content, and can send email or post is all three. Willison argues that guardrails which catch most attacks are not enough: "in web application security 95% is very much a failing grade." Architect the exfiltration path away rather than trusting a prompt to behave. (The Founder Stack's [operator-lessons.md](https://github.com/savvides/edtechfounderstack/blob/800fcaf5fcc187b3ee3c35f4224cbcdc560548a6/data/operator-lessons.md) paraphrases Willison's podcast version of this point, under its own license terms.)
- Whether AI is your product's load-bearing wall or a bolted-on chatbot changes how much of this risk you own. See [ai-native-framework.md](ai-native-framework.md). The deeper the AI sits in the product, the more the cognition and trust questions on this page are yours to answer, not the model lab's.
- What stops a competitor from copying the product is a separate question. See [defensibility-moats.md](defensibility-moats.md).
