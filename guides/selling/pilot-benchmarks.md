# Pilot design benchmarks

Plan a first pilot of 8 to 12 weeks, with success criteria the buyer has agreed to in writing before launch. Most numbers on this page are practitioner estimates; the table below says which.

**Applies to:** K-12 and higher ed, United States. Participant counts use classrooms, a K-12 unit. For a higher-ed pilot meant to lead to a purchase, the [EdTech pilot playbook](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md) is the method; this page supplies the numbers around it. For advising and student-success tools, see [Advising, retention, and persistence pilots](#advising-retention-and-persistence-pilots).

## Timeline

| Length | What it can show (practitioner estimate) |
|---|---|
| 4-6 weeks | Usability, engagement, and teacher or faculty adoption. Too short for outcome data. Suits a proof of concept. |
| 8-12 weeks | Behavior change and preliminary outcomes, within one semester. The usual choice. |
| Full semester (15-16 weeks) | A pre/post assessment with enough instructional time to show an effect. Institutional research teams expect this length for rigorous evidence. |
| Full academic year | Outcomes measured once a year, such as fall-to-fall retention. Uncommon for a first pilot. |

Pilots that lead to contracts most often run 8 to 12 weeks (practitioner estimate). Shorter pilots produce enthusiasm without evidence; longer ones lose momentum.

No evidence tier requires a full year. Neither the ESSA definition ([20 U.S.C. 7801(21)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/7801)) nor the Department of Education's grant definitions ([34 CFR 77.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/34/77.1)) sets a minimum study length. See [evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md) for what each tier does require.

The playbook's structured pilot runs [12 weeks](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#how-to-run-a-pilot-in-12-weeks), the top of the usual range. Its fourth [hard filter](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#hard-filters-passfail) asks for measurable evidence within the 12-18 month sales cycle, a longer window than the pilot. Outcomes measured in a later term, such as retention, arrive in that window.

## Participant scale

| Purpose | Size (practitioner estimate) |
|---|---|
| Qualitative insight | 2-3 classrooms (50-75 students) |
| Quantitative analysis | 5-10 classrooms (150-300 students) |
| Rigorous study | 10 or more classrooms across several schools or departments, with a comparison group |

Start with 3 to 5 classrooms: enough data to show patterns, few enough to support well.

**Higher ed.** These ranges assume 25 to 30 students per classroom, so 3 to 5 classrooms is roughly 75 to 150 students. That suits a first pilot about usability and adoption. For a structured higher-ed pilot that compares outcomes, the playbook asks for a [cohort of 200 or more students](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#week-1-2-design), inside the quantitative range above. Both numbers are practitioner thresholds. Whether a cohort is large enough depends on the size of the effect you need to detect and on the study design.

## Success metrics that matter

**Engagement shows the pilot ran.** Track the share of enrolled users who use the product regularly, session frequency and duration, feature adoption, and the volume and kind of support tickets.

Practitioner estimate: a healthy pilot shows 60-80% weekly active usage among enrolled participants, and usage below 40% points to a failure of implementation more often than of the product. These figures came into the library with Founder Stack's first version, which cites no source. They are not measured rates. Agree on a usage target with the buyer before launch, based on how often the product is meant to be used.

**Outcomes are what buyers pay for.** Candidates:

- Pre/post assessment scores on the targeted skill or knowledge
- Assignment completion rates
- Course grades in pilot sections against non-pilot sections
- Time to competency for a specific skill
- Learners' self-reported confidence or satisfaction

Practitioner estimate: meaningful pilots show 10-25% improvement on the targeted outcome against a baseline or comparison group; effects under 5% are hard to tell from noise; effects over 30% deserve a check for measurement bias. These figures also came from Founder Stack's first version, which cites no source and does not say whether the percentages are relative changes or percentage points.

Education research reports effects in standard deviations (SD), so those percentages cannot be compared directly with published studies. For causal studies of preK-12 interventions with standardized achievement outcomes, [Kraft (2020)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X20912798) proposes treating less than 0.05 SD as small, 0.05 to less than 0.20 SD as medium, and 0.20 SD or more as large. He bases these on 1,942 effect sizes from 747 randomized controlled trials, with a median of 0.10 SD. Across 645 studies of preschool, reading, mathematics, and science programs, [Cheung and Slavin (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0013189X16656615) found effect sizes roughly twice as large in small-scale trials and with experimenter-made measures as in large-scale studies and independent measures. Neither paper covers higher-ed outcomes. ScaleU's view: expect a small pilot scored on your own assessment to overstate the effect a buyer will see at scale.

**Teachers and faculty decide whether use continues.** Measure time saved on specific tasks (grading, feedback, content creation), self-reported satisfaction and willingness to continue, and changes in instructional practice.

## Why pilots fail

These are practitioner observations, not rechecked against a source.

1. **No champion.** The pilot was mandated, and nobody volunteered. Adoption suffered.
2. **Thin onboarding.** Nobody showed teachers or faculty how to fit the tool into instruction, and they used a fraction of it.
3. **Wrong metrics.** The pilot measured engagement and the buyer wanted outcomes, or it measured outcomes on the wrong assessment.
4. **Too much ambition.** One pilot tried to prove everything. Choose one or two outcomes.
5. **No comparison.** Without a baseline or comparison group, the pilot yields an anecdote ("students liked it") where the buyer needed evidence ("pilot sections scored higher than comparison sections").
6. **Inconsistent use.** Each teacher used the product differently, so the pilot had no single treatment to evaluate.

## Advising, retention, and persistence pilots

This section applies to higher ed. Advising and student-success tools serve the underserved Onboard and Select & Enroll phases of the [higher-ed jobs atlas](../demand/higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md): the post-admission dead zone (Job 7), course selection (Job 9), sequencing and advising (Job 11), and the degree audit (Job 12). Name the job your tool does before you design the pilot.

**Each stakeholder needs different evidence.** The Cracking Higher Ed framework uses a retention analytics tool as its example ([SXSW EDU 2026](../../sources/events/2026-sxsw-edu/README.md#3-identify-the-buyer-not-just-the-user)):

| Stakeholder | What they need from a pilot |
|---|---|
| Academic advisor | Operational dashboard proving time savings |
| VP of Student Affairs | Outcome data showing measurable improvement |
| Provost | Year-over-year retention numbers |

The advisor is the easiest to reach and does not control the budget. Design the pilot to produce the evidence the budget holder needs, and map the rest of the approval chain with [demand validation](../demand/demand-validation.md) question 5.

**A 12-week pilot cannot measure retention.** NCES defines the retention rate as the percentage of first-time undergraduates who return to the same institution the following fall ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/ctr)). The National Student Clearinghouse counts students as persisted if they remain enrolled at any institution and as retained if they remain at their starting institution. Its earliest measure is the next spring term ([Persistence and Retention methodological notes, June 2026](https://nscresearchcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/PersistenceandRetention2026_MethodNotes.pdf)). A one-term pilot can therefore show only measures that move within the term: advisor time per student, the share of students reached, or next-term registration. Agree before launch which of these counts as success, and who will report persistence and retention when those numbers arrive.

**Treat retention as a symptom.** Cracking Higher Ed warns that a retention intervention can miss the upstream decision where the problem began ([Patterns founders miss](../../sources/events/2026-sxsw-edu/README.md#7-patterns-founders-miss)). Trace the drop-off back to a job in the atlas before choosing your outcome metric.

**Published evidence on advising.** The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide [Effective Advising for Postsecondary Students](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/28) (October 2021) makes four recommendations. It rates the evidence as strong for three: sustained, personalized relationships; mentoring and coaching; and positive incentives for participation. It rates the evidence as moderate for comprehensive, integrated advising. The guide reviews advising practices, not specific software. Check whether the studies behind a recommendation match your tool and population before citing them in your logic model (see [evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md)).

## Pilot agreement (MOU) elements

Put the pilot in writing before launch. The [pilot agreement template](../templates/pilot-agreement.md) is a starting draft. A memorandum of understanding should cover these ten elements (practitioner checklist):

1. **Objectives.** The specific questions the pilot will answer.
2. **Timeline.** Start date, end date, and milestones.
3. **Participants.** Number of classrooms or sections, and how they are selected.
4. **Implementation plan.** Training schedule, support availability, and expected usage.
5. **Data collection.** What is collected, by whom, how it is stored, and how it is shared.
6. **Success criteria.** Specific, measurable, and agreed in advance.
7. **Data privacy.** FERPA terms, data governance, and what happens to the data after the pilot. See [vendor review readiness](vendor-review-readiness.md).
8. **Costs.** Who pays for product access, researcher time, and assessments.
9. **Intellectual property and publication.** Who owns the findings, and whether results may be published or shared.
10. **Exit plan.** The adoption path, data deletion, and transition at the end.

## IRB considerations

Review by an institutional review board (IRB) is likely required when the pilot collects data about people for research, that is, to produce generalizable knowledge. It may not be required when the pilot is purely internal quality improvement or product evaluation. The institution's IRB office makes the call; ask it.

- **Publishing or marketing the results.** If you plan either, seek IRB approval. It adds credibility and protects you legally (practitioner advice).
- **Many classroom studies qualify for exemption.** Under the federal Common Rule, the exemption categories include research in established educational settings on normal educational practices that are not likely to adversely affect students' opportunity to learn ([45 CFR 46.104(d)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/46.104)), and research that only uses educational tests, surveys, interviews, or observation of public behavior, subject to conditions on identifiability and risk (46.104(d)(2)). Expedited review is for minimal-risk research in categories on a federal list ([45 CFR 46.110](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/46.110)). Ask the IRB office which applies to your study.
- **Review takes weeks.** Across AAHRPP-accredited academic institutions, the 2025 medians were 15 days from submission to an exemption determination, 32 days from submission to approval under expedited review, and 56 days from submission to approval by the convened board. The expedited and convened-board figures cover institutions with their own IRB ([AAHRPP 2025 metrics for academic institutions](https://www.aahrpp.org/docs/default-source/metrics/2025-metrics-for-academic-organizations.pdf), September 2026). Half of submissions take longer than the median, so ask the IRB office for its own turnaround.
- **Who submits.** A university research partner, if you have one, handles the submission.

## Pilot-to-contract conversion

These are practitioner estimates, not measured rates. They came into the library with Founder Stack's first version, which cites no study, dataset, or sample size. A search on October 30, 2026, found no published source that reports such rates. The closest published study, [Piloting Ed-tech Products in K-12 Public Schools](https://digitalpromise.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/UCDavis_EdTechPilotReport.pdf) (UC Davis for Digital Promise, 2015, six K-12 districts), reports no conversion rate. It found that the districts defined pilot success differently.

| Pilot | Conversion to a paid contract (estimate) |
|---|---|
| Well run | 40-60% |
| With a clear faculty or teacher champion | 70% or more |
| Mandated by administration, no champion | 20-30% |
| With success criteria agreed in advance | Higher, because the basis for the decision already exists |

Four practices raise the odds (practitioner view):

- Present results in terms of the buyer's priorities.
- Have an adoption path ready before the pilot ends.
- Show what the product would look like at scale, not only that it worked in the pilot.
- Raise implementation concerns (training, support, integration) before the buyer does.
