# Pilot Design Benchmarks

Data and patterns from edtech pilots at research universities and K-12 districts. Use these benchmarks to design effective pilots and set realistic expectations.

**Who this applies to:** K-12 and higher-ed founders. The participant counts use classrooms, a K-12 unit. If you are designing a higher-ed pilot that should lead to a purchase, use the [EdTech Pilot Playbook](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md) as the method. This page adds benchmarks around it and links to it rather than repeating it. For advising and student-success tools, see [Advising, retention, and persistence pilots](#advising-retention-and-persistence-pilots).

## What makes a pilot succeed

### Timeline

- **4-6 weeks:** Too short for meaningful outcome data. Can demonstrate usability, engagement, and teacher/faculty adoption. Acceptable for a proof-of-concept but not for evidence building.
- **8-12 weeks:** The sweet spot for most edtech pilots. Long enough to measure behavior change and preliminary outcomes. Short enough to stay within a single semester.
- **Full semester (15-16 weeks):** Required for rigorous evidence. Allows pre/post assessment with enough instructional time for impact. This is what institutional research teams expect.
- **Full academic year:** Required for Tier 1-2 evidence. Uncommon for initial pilots.

**Benchmark:** Pilots that lead to adoption contracts are most commonly 8-12 weeks. Shorter pilots generate enthusiasm but not evidence. Longer pilots lose momentum.

**How this fits the playbook:** The playbook's structured pilot runs [12 weeks](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#how-to-run-a-pilot-in-12-weeks), the top of this range. Its fourth [hard filter](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#hard-filters-passfail) asks for measurable evidence within the 12-18 month sales cycle, not within the pilot. Outcomes measured in a later term, such as retention, arrive in that longer window.

### Participant scale

- **Minimum viable:** 2-3 classrooms (50-75 students) for qualitative insights
- **Statistically meaningful:** 5-10 classrooms (150-300 students) for quantitative analysis
- **Rigorous study:** 10+ classrooms across multiple schools/departments with comparison group

**Benchmark:** Start with 3-5 classrooms. This gives you enough data to identify patterns while being manageable for support and implementation.

**Higher ed:** These ranges assume about 25-30 students per classroom, so 3-5 classrooms is roughly 75-150 students. That suits a first pilot about usability and adoption. For a structured higher-ed pilot that compares outcomes, the playbook asks for a [cohort of 200 or more students](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#week-1-2-design), within the "statistically meaningful" range above. Both numbers are practitioner thresholds. Whether a cohort is large enough depends on the size of the effect you need to detect and on the study design.

### Success metrics that matter

**Engagement metrics (necessary but not sufficient):**
- Active usage rate (% of enrolled users who actually use the product regularly)
- Session frequency and duration
- Feature adoption patterns
- Support ticket volume and nature

**Benchmark — active usage:** A healthy pilot shows 60-80% weekly active usage among enrolled participants. Below 40% indicates an adoption problem (not a product problem — an implementation problem).

**Outcome metrics (what buyers actually care about):**
- Pre/post assessment scores on targeted skills/knowledge
- Assignment completion rates
- Course grade comparison (pilot vs. non-pilot sections)
- Time-to-competency for specific skills
- Student/learner self-reported confidence or satisfaction

**Benchmark — outcome improvement:** Meaningful pilots show 10-25% improvement on targeted outcomes compared to baseline or comparison. Effects smaller than 5% are hard to distinguish from noise. Effects larger than 30% should be scrutinized for measurement bias.

**Teacher/faculty metrics:**
- Time saved on specific tasks (grading, feedback, content creation)
- Self-reported satisfaction and willingness to continue
- Changes in instructional practice

### Common reasons pilots fail

1. **No faculty/teacher champion:** The pilot was mandated, not volunteered for. Adoption suffers.
2. **Insufficient onboarding:** Teachers/faculty weren't trained on how to integrate the tool into their instruction. They used 10% of the features.
3. **Wrong metrics:** The pilot measured engagement but the buyer wanted outcome data. Or the pilot measured outcomes on the wrong assessment.
4. **Too ambitious:** Trying to prove too much in one pilot. Focus on one or two clear outcomes.
5. **No comparison data:** Without a comparison group or baseline, positive results are anecdotal ("students liked it") not evidence ("students in pilot sections scored 15% higher").
6. **Implementation variability:** Different teachers used the product completely differently. The "treatment" wasn't consistent enough to evaluate.

## Advising, retention, and persistence pilots

This section applies to higher ed. Advising and student-success tools serve the underserved Onboard and Select & Enroll phases of the [higher-ed jobs atlas](../demand/higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md): the post-admission dead zone (Job 7), course selection (Job 9), sequencing and advising (Job 11), and the degree audit (Job 12). Name the job your tool does before you design the pilot.

**Each stakeholder needs different evidence.** The Cracking Higher Ed framework uses a retention analytics tool as its example ([SXSW EDU 2026](../../sources/events/2026-sxsw-edu/README.md#3-identify-the-buyer-not-just-the-user)):

| Stakeholder | What they need from a pilot |
|---|---|
| Academic advisor | Operational dashboard proving time savings |
| VP of Student Affairs | Outcome data showing measurable improvement |
| Provost | Year-over-year retention numbers |

The advisor is the easiest to reach but doesn't control the budget. Design the pilot so it produces the evidence the budget holder needs, and map the rest of the approval chain with [demand validation](../demand/demand-validation.md) question 5.

**A 12-week pilot can't measure retention.** NCES defines the retention rate as the percentage of first-time undergraduates who return to the same institution the following fall ([NCES](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/ctr)). The National Student Clearinghouse counts students as *persisted* if they remain enrolled at any institution and *retained* if they remain at their starting institution. Its earliest measure is the next spring term ([Persistence and Retention methodological notes, June 2026](https://nscresearchcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/PersistenceandRetention2026_MethodNotes.pdf)). So a one-term pilot can show only measures that move within the term, such as advisor time per student, the share of students reached, or next-term registration. Agree before launch which of these counts as success, and who will report persistence and retention when those numbers become available.

**Treat retention as a symptom.** Cracking Higher Ed warns that a retention intervention can miss the upstream decision where the problem began ([Patterns founders miss](../../sources/events/2026-sxsw-edu/README.md#7-patterns-founders-miss)). Trace the drop-off back to a job in the atlas before choosing your outcome metric.

**Published evidence on advising.** The What Works Clearinghouse practice guide [Effective Advising for Postsecondary Students](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/WWC/PracticeGuide/28) (October 2021) makes four recommendations. It rates the evidence as strong for three: sustained, personalized relationships; mentoring and coaching; and positive incentives for participation. It rates the evidence as moderate for comprehensive, integrated advising. The guide reviews advising practices, not specific software. Check whether the studies behind a recommendation match your tool and population before citing them in your logic model (see [evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md)).

## Pilot Agreement (MOU) Elements

A Memorandum of Understanding for an edtech pilot should cover:

1. **Objectives:** What specific questions will this pilot answer?
2. **Timeline:** Start date, end date, key milestones
3. **Participants:** Number of classrooms/sections, selection criteria
4. **Implementation plan:** Training schedule, support availability, expected usage
5. **Data collection:** What data will be collected, by whom, how stored, how shared
6. **Success criteria:** Specific, measurable criteria agreed upon in advance
7. **Data privacy:** FERPA compliance, data governance, what happens to data after pilot
8. **Costs:** Who pays for what (product access, researcher time, assessment costs)
9. **IP and publication:** Who owns the findings, can results be published/shared
10. **Exit plan:** What happens at the end — adoption path, data deletion, transition

## IRB Considerations

**When is IRB review needed?**
- If the pilot involves collecting data about human subjects for research purposes (i.e., to generate generalizable knowledge), IRB review is likely required
- If the pilot is purely for internal quality improvement or product evaluation, IRB may not be required
- When in doubt, consult the institution's IRB office — they'll tell you

**Practical guidance:**
- If you plan to publish results or use findings in marketing, get IRB approval. It adds credibility and protects you legally.
- IRB review takes 2-8 weeks for expedited review, 1-3 months for full board review
- Most edtech pilots qualify for expedited review (minimal risk to participants)
- Your university research partner (if you have one) handles the IRB submission

## Pilot-to-Contract Conversion

**Practitioner estimates, not measured rates.** These figures came into the library with the first version of EdTech Founder Stack (March 31, 2026). That version described its benchmarks as directional patterns from accelerator programs and university partnerships. It cites no study, dataset, or sample size, and a search on October 30, 2026 found no published source that reports these rates. The closest published study, [Piloting Ed-tech Products in K-12 Public Schools](https://digitalpromise.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/UCDavis_EdTechPilotReport.pdf) (UC Davis for Digital Promise, 2015, six K-12 districts), reports no conversion rate. It found that the districts defined pilot success differently.

**Estimates:**
- Well-run pilots convert to paid contracts 40-60% of the time
- Pilots with a clear faculty/teacher champion convert at 70%+
- Pilots mandated by administration (no champion) convert at 20-30%
- Pilots with pre-agreed success criteria convert at higher rates because the decision framework already exists

**What increases conversion:**
- Presenting results in the buyer's language (not your metrics, their priorities)
- Having a clear adoption path ready before the pilot ends
- Showing not just "it worked" but "here's what it would look like at scale"
- Addressing implementation concerns proactively (training, support, integration)

## Readiness filters and the decision gate

The [EdTech Pilot Playbook](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md) is the canonical version of both. Before a structured pilot, clear its five pass/fail [hard filters](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#hard-filters-passfail). While designing the pilot, agree in advance what result means go, pivot, or no-go ([weeks 1-2](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#week-1-2-design)). At the end, apply that [decision gate](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#week-11-12-evidence) and share the evidence, including negative findings.
