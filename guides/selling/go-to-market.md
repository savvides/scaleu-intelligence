# Go-to-market: sales motions and channels

Pick one motion that matches your price and your buyer, make it repeat, and add a second only when the first produces renewals. Most channels on this page shorten a sale you have already won; few of them create demand.

**Applies to:** K-12 and higher ed, with pointers for consumer. United States.

## The motions compared

Price bands and cycle lengths are practitioner estimates. Use the [procurement guide](procurement-guide.md) for purchase thresholds and the [budget calendar](procurement-guide.md#the-budget-calendar), which set the timing for every row below.

| Motion | Who signs | Fits annual price (estimate) | Cycle (estimate) | What it requires | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom-up: teachers or faculty adopt, then a school, district, or department pays | Principal, department chair, later district or provost | Under $25,000 per account at first | Weeks to adopt, 6–18 months to convert | Self-serve onboarding, usage data by school, a paid tier an administrator wants | Free use that never reaches a budget holder |
| Top-down enterprise sale | District cabinet, CIO, provost | $25,000 and up | 6–18 months | A seller, a pilot, [review paperwork](vendor-review-readiness.md), references | Running out of cash before the first renewal |
| State-level sale or adoption list | State agency or board | Six or seven figures, or a listing with no revenue attached | 1–3 years | Evidence, staff for a formal review, patience through a political process | Treating a listing as a sale |
| Reseller or channel partner | The partner's customer | Any, minus the partner's margin | Depends on the partner's attention | A product a rep can explain in two minutes, partner training, margin | Signing a partner who never sells it |
| Cooperative or piggyback contract | Any member agency | Deals above the buyer's bid threshold | Months to win the contract, then faster deals | Winning a competitive solicitation, fee reporting | Winning the contract and waiting for orders |
| Marketplace or platform program | The platform's customer | Any | Weeks to list | The integration itself ([interoperability](../product/interoperability.md)) | Expecting the listing to generate leads |
| Direct to consumer, parent-paid | Parent or learner | Under a few hundred dollars per year | Minutes | Paid acquisition or organic reach, retention | Acquisition cost above lifetime value |

## Bottom-up adoption

Teachers and faculty can try a tool without asking anyone, which makes this the cheapest way to get usage and the least certain way to get revenue. The [higher-ed guide](../market/highered-landscape.md#selling-to-higher-ed) makes the same point about faculty autonomy.

The motion works when three things are true. An administrator gets something the free version withholds: rostering, single sign-on, school-level reporting, a signed data privacy agreement. Usage clusters, so you can show a principal that 14 of her teachers already use the product. And the first paid step sits under the buyer's informal purchase threshold.

The common failure is free use that never converts. ScaleU's view: it has four usual causes. The free tier already does the whole job. The teacher has no budget, and nobody with a budget feels the problem. Usage is spread one teacher per school, so no single account is worth a call. Or the district blocks the product once its privacy review catches up with what teachers installed. A search on November 11, 2026, found no public dataset on freemium conversion rates in edtech, so treat any percentage you are quoted as anecdote. The nearest hard evidence is on the other side of the sale: a Glimpse K12 analysis of about 200,000 curriculum software licenses bought by 275 schools in 2017–18 found that 67% went unused ([EdWeek Market Brief, 2019](https://marketbrief.edweek.org/marketplace-k-12/unused-educational-software-major-source-wasted-k-12-spending-new-analysis-finds/)). The sample is small and old, but the lesson holds in both directions: adoption without purchase earns nothing, and purchase without use does not renew.

Decide before launch what event triggers a sales conversation (for example, five active teachers in one school), and measure conversion from that event, not from sign-ups. Test demand first with the methods in [demand validation](../demand/demand-validation.md).

## Top-down enterprise sales

Selling to the district or institution is slow and expensive, and it is the only motion that reliably produces contracts above $25,000. Expect a pilot ([pilot playbook](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md)), a security and privacy review, and a budget cycle. Founders should run the first ten of these sales themselves: the objections you hear are product research. Hire a seller once you can write down why the last five deals closed.

## State-level sales and adoption lists

A state can be a customer, a gatekeeper, or both. As a customer, a state agency buys a statewide license, usually by RFP. As a gatekeeper, it publishes a list that districts consult or must use.

Texas shows how formal the gatekeeper role can be. The State Board of Education's Instructional Materials Review and Approval process, created by House Bill 1605, calls for materials through a Request for Instructional Materials. The 2027 cycle covers subjects including K-12 mathematics, English and Spanish language arts, career and technical education, fine arts, and languages other than English. Its request was published June 30, 2026, and publisher interest forms closed September 2, 2026 ([SBOE publisher page](https://sboe.texas.gov/state-board-of-education/imra/imra-publishers)). Miss a window and you wait a year.

These lists mostly cover core and supplemental curriculum. Newer state lists review AI tools; see [state AI requirements](../market/k12-regulatory.md#state-ai-requirements). In either case a listing is permission to sell, and each district still has to be sold.

## Resellers and channel partners

A reseller brings contract vehicles, a vendor number in hundreds of districts, and reps who already visit your buyer. In exchange it takes a margin (practitioner estimate: often 10–30%) and gives you a small share of each rep's attention. Use a reseller when a buyer asks to purchase through one, or when you already have demand in a region you cannot staff. A partner will fulfill demand you created; it will rarely create demand for an unknown product.

## Purchasing cooperatives and piggyback contracts

A cooperative runs one competitive solicitation and lets its member agencies buy from the winners without bidding again; the procurement guide explains [the rules that allow it](procurement-guide.md#cooperative-and-piggyback-contracts). For a buyer, that turns a formal bid into a purchase order. For you, it removes the bid and leaves the sale. Each state decides whether its districts may use a given cooperative, so ask the buyer's purchasing office which contracts it accepts before you bid on one.

| Cooperative | Who runs it and who buys | What the vendor does |
|---|---|---|
| [Sourcewell](https://www.sourcewell-mn.gov/cooperative-purchasing/how-it-works) | A Minnesota unit of government; government and education entities register free | Win a national RFP |
| [OMNIA Partners](https://www.omniapartners.com/publicsector/about-us/who-we-are) | Nearly 1,000 contracts across K-12, higher ed, and government; free membership | Hold one of the underlying contracts |
| [BuyBoard](https://www.buyboard.com/vendor/vendor-faq) | Administered by the Texas Association of School Boards; open to local governments and nonprofits | Respond to a proposal invitation; pay a 2% service fee on each purchase order |
| TIPS (The Interlocal Purchasing System) | Run by Region 8 Education Service Center in Texas, separate from TASB | Win a TIPS solicitation; pay a fee on sales |
| [Texas DIR cooperative contracts](https://dir.texas.gov/cooperative-contracts) | State IT contracts open to Texas K-12 (public, private, charter), higher ed, and out-of-state public entities with an interstate agreement ([eligibility](https://dir.texas.gov/it-solutions-and-services/customer-eligibility)); DIR reports $599.9M in K-12 purchases in 2024 | Win a DIR solicitation, attend contract training, report sales monthly, and pay administrative fees ([vendor page](https://dir.texas.gov/it-solutions-and-services/selling-through-dir)) |
| [AEPA](https://aepacoop.org/) | 31 state agencies; bids are national and awarded state by state | Bid in AEPA's annual cycle |
| [E&I Cooperative Services](https://www.eandi.org/company/about-us/) | Nonprofit, member-owned, education only; more than 6,500 member institutions and 260 contracts | Win an E&I solicitation |
| [MHEC](https://www.mhec.org/contracts) | Midwestern Higher Education Compact; contracts open to colleges in member and sister-compact states, and most technology contracts to K-12 and local government | Win a MHEC RFP |

Cooperatives often ask for several years of financial history, which the [procurement guide](procurement-guide.md) notes few young companies have. Before you have that history, sell through a reseller that already holds a cooperative contract. ScaleU's view: bid on your own contract when at least three buyers have told you they would have purchased sooner with one.

## Marketplaces and platform programs

A listing puts you where an administrator already manages apps. It proves the integration works and gives the technology director less to do. It does not bring buyers to you.

| Program | What it requires | Cost to the vendor |
|---|---|---|
| [Clever](https://clever.com/pricing) | Single sign-on through the Clever Library; rostering through Secure Sync | Library integration is free; rostering is a custom quote. Clever is free to schools and bars partners from charging schools for it |
| [ClassLink](https://classlink.com/oneroster) | OneRoster rostering; SAML, OAuth, or LTI sign-on | ClassLink states it charges vendors no fee for sign-on support |
| [Instructure partner program](https://www.instructure.com/partners) | An interest form; options for platform integration, content, and evidence services | Terms are not published; ask |
| [Google Classroom add-ons](https://developers.google.com/workspace/classroom/add-ons) | Build the add-on, pass Google's review, publish in the Google Workspace Marketplace | Only teachers with a Teaching & Learning or Plus license can use add-ons, so reach is limited to paid editions |
| [Microsoft Marketplace](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/partner-center/marketplace-offers/marketplace-faq-publisher-guide) | Accept the Microsoft Partner and Publisher agreements; publish through Partner Center | No listing fee; 3% on transactable offers |
| [AWS Marketplace](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/marketplace/latest/userguide/user-guide-for-sellers.html) | Seller registration; for paid products, tax forms and a bank account | [Listing fees](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/marketplace/latest/userguide/listing-fees.html): 3% on public SaaS offers; private offers 3% under $1M, 2% from $1M to under $10M, 1.5% at $10M and above and on renewals |

Do Clever and ClassLink first if you sell to K-12 districts: an IT director's first question is whether the product rosters automatically. The cloud marketplaces matter in higher ed and workforce, where a buyer can pay you through an existing Microsoft or AWS agreement and skip new-vendor setup.

## Conferences

A conference is a channel when you arrive with meetings booked. A booth without meetings buys badge scans. For which events serve which buyer, see [resources](../resources.md).

## Higher ed: system offices and consortia

A public university system office can sign a master agreement that lets each campus buy without its own RFP. So can a consortium: E&I and MHEC appear above, and [Internet2 NET+](https://internet2.edu/cloud/internet2-net-plus-services/) has peer institutions vet a cloud service's security, pricing, and contract terms once for all subscribers. ScaleU's view: system offices rarely buy first. Win two or three campuses, then ask the system to make purchasing easier for the rest. A master agreement mandates nothing, so each campus remains its own sale.

## Direct to consumer

Selling to parents and adult learners avoids procurement and replaces it with acquisition cost and churn. The mechanics differ enough to have their own guide: [consumer learning](../market/consumer-learning.md).

## Choosing a first motion

Start from the price your product can command and the person who feels the problem (practitioner view; see [business models and pricing](../business/business-models-and-pricing.md)).

1. Under about $5,000 per school, with a teacher as daily user: bottom-up, with a school-level paid plan. No field seller pays for itself at this price.
2. $5,000 to $25,000, with a principal, department, or program director as buyer: founder-led direct sales, priced below the buyer's bid threshold.
3. Above $25,000, with a district or institution as buyer: top-down sales with a pilot, and the review paperwork ready before the first demo.
4. Parent or learner pays: direct to consumer.

If the user and the budget holder are different people and you cannot name the budget holder, you have a [demand problem](../demand/founder-traps.md), and no motion will fix it.

## Adding a second motion

Add a second motion when the first renews, and choose the one your customers are already asking for.

- Bottom-up to top-down: when several schools in one district pay separately, offer the district one contract. Your usage data is the pitch.
- Direct to cooperative or reseller: when buyers say yes and then stall on purchasing mechanics.
- One state to the next: when you have references and the next state's privacy terms signed. See [vendor review readiness](vendor-review-readiness.md).
- School to consumer, or the reverse: rarely. The product, price, and marketing differ enough that it amounts to a second company.
