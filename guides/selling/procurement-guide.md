# Procurement guide: how schools and universities buy

The price you quote decides the process your buyer must follow, and the process decides how long the deal takes. Learn your buyer's dollar thresholds and budget calendar before you set a price or a close date.

**Applies to:** K-12 and higher ed, United States. Public institutions follow state law and board policy; private ones set their own rules. Every threshold below is an example. Your buyer's written policy decides.

## Purchase thresholds

Each buyer has two or three dollar lines. Below the first, a department can buy on its own. Between the lines, purchasing collects quotes. Above the last, the purchase goes to a public solicitation. The lines sit in different places for different buyers:

| Buyer and rule | Small purchases | Quotes | Formal solicitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Any school or college spending federal grant money ([2 CFR 200.320](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.320)) | Up to the micro-purchase threshold, $15,000: no quotes if the price is reasonable. A recipient may self-certify a threshold up to $50,000. | Up to the simplified acquisition threshold, $350,000: quotes from an adequate number of qualified sources | Above $350,000: sealed bids or proposals with public notice |
| University of Arizona ([procurement policy 4.4](https://policy.arizona.edu/procurement-policy/ppm-44-request-bids-rfb-proposals-rfp), revised February 25, 2024) | $25,000 or less | Over $25,000 to $100,000: informal solicitation sent to at least three vendors | Over $100,000: sealed competitive solicitation |
| Ohio University ([policy 55.007](https://www.ohio.edu/policy/55-007), effective 2007) | Below the bid amounts | Not stated in this policy | Competitive bid at $25,000 or more for goods, or $50,000 or more for services, paid to one vendor in a fiscal year |
| Texas school districts ([Education Code 44.031(a)](https://capitol.texas.gov/tlodocs/89R/billtext/html/SB01173F.htm), as amended by S.B. 1173, effective September 1, 2025) | No state requirement below $100,000; local policy may add one ([Texas Education Agency, November 6, 2025](https://tea.texas.gov/about-tea/news-and-multimedia/correspondence/taa-letters/federal-grant-threshold-changes-update)) | Set by local policy | $100,000 or more in aggregate over 12 months: competitive bidding, sealed proposals, an RFP, an interlocal contract, or another listed method. The line was $50,000 before the amendment. |
| California school districts ([California Department of Education](https://www.cde.ca.gov/ls/fa/bidding.asp); figure as reported by [Lozano Smith](https://lozanosmith.com/news-clientnewsbriefdetail.php?news_id=3451)) | Set by district policy | Set by district policy | Above $119,100 for equipment, materials, supplies, and non-construction services, effective January 1, 2026. The state adjusts the figure each January. |

Four points follow from the table.

1. **Federal money carries federal rules.** 2 CFR 200.320 names no dollar amounts. It points to the definitions in [2 CFR 200.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.1), which adopt the thresholds in the Federal Acquisition Regulation ([FAR 2.101](https://www.acquisition.gov/far/2.101)): $15,000 and $350,000 since October 1, 2025, up from $10,000 and $250,000 ([final rule, August 27, 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/08/27/2025-16412/federal-acquisition-regulation-inflation-adjustment-of-acquisition-related-thresholds)). A recipient may set either threshold lower. It may never set the simplified acquisition threshold higher; it may raise the micro-purchase threshold to $50,000 by certifying its own eligibility each year, and above $50,000 only with federal approval (2 CFR 200.320(a)). When state and federal rules both apply, the stricter one governs, as the Texas Education Agency letter above tells its districts.
2. **Arizona draws its line at $100,000.** The state procurement code lets agencies use simplified rules for procurements that do not exceed $100,000 ([A.R.S. 41-2535](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/41/02535.htm)). The Board of Regents sets the same figure for the state universities and asks that purchases under it go to small businesses where practicable ([ASU purchasing manual, PUR 104](https://public.powerdms.com/ASU/documents/1559187), revised November 1, 2020).
3. **Thresholds count total spend.** Texas counts purchases in aggregate over 12 months, Ohio University counts payments to one vendor in a fiscal year, and ASU's manual forbids dividing a requirement to avoid the source-selection rules. A renewal or a second department's order can push you over the line.
4. **Some purchases are exempt.** Ohio's school-district bidding statute covers building work and lists instructional software among its exceptions ([Ohio Revised Code 3313.46](https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-revised-code/section-3313.46), version effective September 30, 2025). Ask the purchasing office which rule covers software subscriptions.

**Practitioner estimate, not data.** Across institutions ScaleU has worked with, purchases under about $5,000 often go on a department purchasing card; purchases from about $5,000 to $25,000 need quotes or a sole-source justification; purchases above about $25,000 draw IT and legal review; and purchases above about $100,000 go to an RFP or the board and take 6 to 12 months. Treat these bands as a first guess and replace them with the buyer's policy.

## Price for the process

- **Ask for the thresholds early.** The playbook's [readiness assessment](edtech-pilot-playbook/README.md#part-2-readiness-assessment) asks whether the institution has a named budget holder, knows its RFP threshold, and has a security review with a known timeline. Ask your champion the same questions.
- **Size the first deal to the faster lane.** A first-year price below the quote or bid line can close in weeks; one just above it can take months (ScaleU's view). Price honestly: because thresholds count aggregate spend, splitting an order to duck the line breaks the buyer's rules and puts your champion at risk.
- **Write the paperwork for your champion.** Hand over a one-page [sole-source justification](../templates/sole-source-justification.md) and a [budget request memo](../templates/budget-request-memo.md) the champion can adapt. Under the federal rules, a noncompetitive award is allowed when only one source can fulfill the need, among other listed circumstances (2 CFR 200.320(c)); state rules differ.
- **Accept purchase orders.** Institutions pay against a purchase order and an invoice. Be able to receive one and bill against it.
- **Offer a multi-year prepaid option.** A discount for three years paid up front gives the institution a predictable budget and gives you cash (ScaleU's view). Expect the non-appropriation clause described under [contract terms](#contract-terms-that-stall-deals).

## The budget calendar

Forty-six states begin their fiscal year on July 1; Alabama, Michigan, New York, and Texas do not ([NCSL, July 2025](https://www.ncsl.org/resources/details/almost-all-states-began-new-fiscal-year-with-enacted-budgets)). In ScaleU's experience most school districts and universities also run a July-to-June year, but confirm your buyer's. The month ranges below are practitioner estimates for a July 1 fiscal year.

**K-12**

| When | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| January to March | Districts plan next year's budget | Be in the conversation before the numbers are set. A first pitch in September is about nine months late for that year's budget. |
| April to June | Purchase orders are issued, and unspent current-year money is committed before the year closes | Be registered as a vendor, with quote and paperwork complete |
| July 1 | The new fiscal year opens | Approved purchases can start; invoices for the old year are due |

**Higher ed**

| When | What happens | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| September to December | Departments and colleges draft requests for the next fiscal year | Run the pilot in the fall term, so results exist when requests are written |
| January to April | Deans and vice presidents review requests; central budget offices set allocations | Give your champion the budget memo and pilot evidence |
| May to June | The board approves the budget; units spend remaining current-year funds | Close small, department-level purchases; finish vendor registration |
| July to August | The new year opens; fall-term tools must be live before classes | Have the contract signed and the integration tested before faculty return |

Institutions enforce year-end cutoffs. Florida Atlantic University's [purchase order terms](https://www.fau.edu/procurement/files/poterms.pdf), for example, require goods and services received by June 30 to be invoiced by June 30.

## How K-12 and higher ed differ

**K-12 buys centrally.** You sell to the district. If the chief technology officer rejects the product over data privacy, the deal ends for every school in the district. ScaleU's view: build demand with the curriculum director, and bring the technology office in early.

**Higher ed buys in pieces.** Departments, and sometimes individual faculty, hold purchasing cards. A vendor can close a dozen $3,000 sales before central IT hears of it. ScaleU's view: land in one department, show outcomes, and take that evidence to the provost for a campus-wide agreement. See [selling to higher ed](../market/highered-landscape.md) and the sales motions in [go-to-market](go-to-market.md).

**Different people want different proof.** In the Cracking Higher Ed framework's example, advisors, student affairs, and the provost each want different evidence from a pilot. Map the job you serve in the [higher-ed jobs atlas](../demand/higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md) to its budget holder, and see [pilot benchmarks](pilot-benchmarks.md#advising-retention-and-persistence-pilots) for student-success pilots.

## Security, privacy, and accessibility review

Review runs alongside purchasing and often takes longer. Many universities ask vendors to complete EDUCAUSE's [HECVAT](https://www.educause.edu/hecvat), a security questionnaire that, since version 4, also asks about privacy and AI. Higher ed will not buy without an accessibility report: have a current VPAT before the first serious meeting. [Vendor review readiness](vendor-review-readiness.md) lists the documents to prepare, and [K-12 regulation](../market/k12-regulatory.md) covers student-privacy law.

## The RFP process

A request for proposals (RFP) is the formal route above the top threshold. Under the federal rules, the buyer must state its evaluation factors and award to the responsible offeror whose proposal is most advantageous, price and other factors considered (2 CFR 200.320(b)(2)). State and institutional rules read much the same; the University of Arizona's policy, for one, bases awards on a comparative evaluation of price, quality, and performance capability, and opens proposals publicly.

The usual sequence (practitioner description; each buyer's documents govern):

1. The buyer posts the RFP on its bid portal and notifies registered vendors.
2. Vendors submit written questions by a deadline; the buyer answers all of them in a public addendum.
3. Vendors submit proposals in the required format by the due date. Late or incomplete proposals are rejected.
4. A committee scores the proposals against the published factors and may invite finalists to demonstrate.
5. The buyer issues a notice of intent to award, negotiates the contract, and, for large awards, takes it to the board.

How to respond (ScaleU's view):

- **Decide whether to bid.** If you first learn of the need from the RFP, a competitor probably helped shape the requirements. Founders at an ASU+GSV 2026 roundtable said that non-winning vendors receive no score and no reason, and that the cost of a proposal is high for the odds ([roundtable memo](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/deliverables/roundtable-memo.md), speaker claims). Early on, prefer deals that begin with a relationship.
- **Register on the bid portal now.** Notices go to registered vendors.
- **Answer in the buyer's order, using the buyer's words.** Scorers work down a rubric. Include every required form.
- **Use the question period.** It is the only sanctioned way to clarify a requirement or challenge one that excludes you.
- **Ask for a debrief if you lose,** and request the winning proposal and score sheets under the state's public records law. Contracts with public institutions are generally public records; Florida Atlantic University's terms say so in plain words.

## Cooperative and piggyback contracts

A cooperative contract is bid once by a lead agency or purchasing cooperative and then used by many buyers, who skip their own solicitation. A piggyback is one institution buying from another's competitively awarded contract, where state law and the contract allow it. For a vendor, one award opens many accounts.

- The federal rules encourage intergovernmental and inter-entity purchasing agreements and say that documented purchases through them meet the competition requirement ([2 CFR 200.318(e)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/2/200.318)).
- Texas lists an interlocal contract among the methods a district may use at or above the $100,000 line (Education Code 44.031(a)). The Texas Education Agency adds that cooperative purchases must still meet the most restrictive rule that applies.
- [Sourcewell](https://www.sourcewell-mn.gov/cooperative-purchasing), a unit of local government in Minnesota, holds hundreds of competitively solicited contracts open to about 50,000 government and education entities.
- In Illinois, the Learning Technology Center runs a free statewide purchasing cooperative and is helping build a national bid for AI tools (see the SETDA findings below).

Getting onto a cooperative contract means winning that cooperative's own solicitation, and the contract opens doors without creating demand. Ask your target buyers which contracts they already use. A reseller that holds one may be the faster route; [go-to-market](go-to-market.md) covers resellers and other channels.

## Contract terms that stall deals

Public institutions sign on their own terms, and many of those terms are fixed by state law. Read the buyer's standard terms before you send your own. Florida Atlantic University's purchase order terms are a representative public example; the clauses below cite them, and other states differ in detail.

| Term | What the institution will ask | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Order of precedence | Its terms override yours. FAU's terms exclude any differing supplier terms. | Keep your terms of service short, and expect an addendum that puts the institution's terms first. |
| Indemnification | You indemnify the institution; it will not indemnify you. FAU cites the state's sovereign immunity. | Do not require customer indemnity from public buyers. Negotiate your own cap, and expect carve-outs for intellectual property claims. |
| Governing law and venue | Its state's law and courts | Accept. |
| Non-appropriation | It may end the contract if the legislature or funder does not provide the money, paying only for services already performed. FAU may also cancel on 30 days' notice without penalty. | Expect this in multi-year deals. Do not count later years as certain revenue. |
| Data ownership and privacy | Its data may be used only for the contract's purpose, may not pass to other parties without written consent, and must be handled under FERPA and breach-notification law. | State in writing what you collect, where it is stored, and how you return or delete it. See [vendor review readiness](vendor-review-readiness.md). |
| Public records | The contract and related correspondence become public records, whatever your confidentiality clause says. | Mark true trade secrets as the state's law requires, and assume your pricing is public. |
| Insurance | A certificate of insurance naming the institution as additional insured, delivered before the contract starts | Ask for the required coverage types and limits early; buying a policy can take weeks. |
| Payment terms | Payment runs from receipt of an acceptable invoice, often 30 to 60 days (the range is a practitioner estimate). FAU's terms allow 40 days before interest may accrue. | Invoice exactly as the purchase order specifies. Plan cash for net-45 or net-60. |
| Vendor registration | A vendor application and an IRS [Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9), which gives the payer your taxpayer identification number. FAU treats an invoice as insufficient for payment until both are on file. | Register as soon as a purchase looks likely. Unregistered vendors cannot be paid. |

## What institutional buyers told us (ASU+GSV 2026)

These are speaker claims from a roundtable of university administrators and founders at the ASU+GSV Summit, April 2026 ([roundtable memo](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/deliverables/roundtable-memo.md)). They are practitioner signals, not research findings.

- **Staff time is the scarce resource.** Administrators said they lack time, more than money, to evaluate, integrate, and run a pilot. ScaleU's view: an institution that cannot find time for your pilot probably does not feel the problem sharply enough yet.
- **Informal pilots precede procurement.** Several administrators run a light test before involving IT or purchasing, to see whether anyone uses the product. Such a test gives an early signal but leaves no documentation and does not transfer to the next institution. One founder preferred results in two weeks to the chance of a $50,000 contract in six months.
- **Buyers trust a peer's method more than a peer's verdict.** An institution rarely accepts another's evidence at face value, because students, context, and infrastructure differ; it will adopt a peer's evaluation protocol. Lead with how you would help them evaluate.
- **Thresholds vary by state.** Participants cited $100,000 for Arizona and $50,000 for Ohio. The verified examples in the table above agree in part: Arizona's figure matches, and Ohio University's bid line is $50,000 for services and $25,000 for goods.
- **RFPs give losers no feedback.** See [the RFP process](#the-rfp-process).

## What state edtech offices told SETDA (K-12, 2026)

SETDA's [2026 State EdTech Trends Report](https://www.setda.org/priorities/state-trends/) surveyed state agency leaders: 73 responses from 45 states and one territory. The respondents are state officials, not district buyers, but state guidance and purchasing cooperatives shape what districts ask of you. Figures are shares of respondents. Details and page numbers are in the [field note](../../sources/notes/2026-10-30-setda-state-edtech-trends-2026.md).

- **States are writing buying standards.** 53% have published guidance on buying AI-enabled tools. That guidance draws on the EdTech Quality Collaborative's [five quality indicators](https://digitalpromise.org/initiative/edtech-quality-collaborative/): safe, evidence-based, inclusive, usable, interoperable. Among states with such guidance, 96% include the safe indicator, and the share including the evidence-based indicator rose from 45% to 79% in a year.
- **Privacy decides first.** Data privacy and security carry the most weight in state purchasing decisions (82%), ahead of evidence of effectiveness (50%) and accessibility, usability, or interoperability (32% each).
- **Vendor claims are discounted.** 68% said it is hard to verify vendor claims about AI products independently. When deciding, states rely on national organizations (62%), peer states (55%), and independent research (48%) well before vendor documentation (28%).
- **Cooperatives and multi-state bids (Illinois, as reported by its staff).** The Learning Technology Center of Illinois runs a free statewide purchasing cooperative and is helping build a national AI-tools bid through the Association of Educational Purchasing Agencies. Traditional procurement looks for three to five years of financial history, which few new AI companies have. Because districts combine funds from several budgets, curriculum coordinators, business officials, and superintendents now sign off on more orders, alongside technology directors.

ScaleU's view: have your privacy package ready before the first demo. Show evidence a reviewer can check without trusting you: your ESSA tier and the study behind it (see [evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md)), a third-party validation if you have one, and a current VPAT. Ask early whether your target districts buy through a cooperative, and what it takes to get on its contract.
