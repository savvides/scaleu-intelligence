# EdTech pilot playbook

A method for running a structured edtech pilot in higher education: decide what you need, check that both sides are ready, run 12 weeks against a written hypothesis, and end with a go, pivot, or no-go decision. It serves institutions evaluating a product and founders building one.

**Applies to:** higher ed, United States and Europe. K-12 founders can use the method, with the classroom-scale numbers in [pilot benchmarks](../pilot-benchmarks.md).

The playbook draws on ScaleU's reviews of more than 4,000 edtech companies, more than 30 paid institutional pilots, and conversations with institutional leaders in the US and Europe. Its thresholds and scores are ScaleU's practice, not research findings. It was first published with ASU+GSV 2026 and is licensed CC BY 4.0, so that any institution can reuse the method.

## The problem this solves

Universities evaluate the same edtech companies independently. Each runs its own discovery, diligence, and pilot design. A company validated at one university runs a nearly identical pilot at the next, and the second institution almost never accepts the first one's evidence.

Both sides pay for the repetition. Institutions spend staff time re-evaluating products their peers have tested. Founders repeat a sales cycle of 12 to 18 months (ScaleU's estimate) at every institution.

This playbook makes the evaluation method, the pilot design, and the readiness criteria public, so that institutions can share them. What to buy remains each institution's decision.

### What we heard from institutional leaders

These are speaker claims from a roundtable of university administrators and edtech founders at ASU+GSV 2026 ([roundtable memo](../../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/deliverables/roundtable-memo.md)).

- **Sourcing.** A vice president of online learning at a public university said she has a list of goals and no time to scan the whole market. She called the RFP process "a gamble," because she cannot be sure it shows her the full slate of vendors.
- **Peer evidence.** An innovation director at a European business school said that successful tests at two or three other universities would help him persuade internal stakeholders to take the risk.
- **Time.** Several leaders named staff time, more than money, as the constraint. Even for a product that asks little of the institution, they lack time to evaluate, integrate, and run a pilot.
- **Lightweight tests.** A few administrators run a quick test before involving IT or procurement. One described setting full technical integration aside, putting the product in front of students, and stopping if nobody uses it.
- **Relationships over RFPs.** Founders called relationship-based selling the only reliable approach at an early stage. Non-winners of an RFP receive no feedback, and a proposal costs a great deal for the odds. A founder of a math and AI tutoring company preferred results and feedback within two weeks to the chance of $50,000 in six months.

## Pilot readiness checklist

Answer these questions before you evaluate a single product. They separate institutions that are ready to pilot from those still deciding what they need.

### Part 1: Needs discovery

Most institutions hear pitches before they have defined what they want, and end up reacting to what vendors offer. Start with four questions.

**1. What specific gap in the student journey are you trying to close?**

Map the student lifecycle: Pre-Enroll, Apply, Onboard, Select & Enroll, Course Experience, Graduate & Beyond. Find the phase with the most friction, where students drop off, struggle, or go unserved. The [higher-ed jobs atlas](../../demand/higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md) lists the jobs in each phase.

If you cannot name a phase and a problem, run a needs assessment before you evaluate products.

**2. What does better look like, and how would you measure it?**

Define the outcome before you see the product. Complete this sentence: "[Target population] who use [intervention] will show [measurable outcome] compared to [comparison group], as measured by [metric] over [time period]."

A pilot without that sentence produces evidence nobody can act on.

**3. Who owns this problem now?**

Name the person, not the department: the one who thinks about this gap daily, who would champion a pilot internally, and who controls or can request the budget.

A pilot that nobody owns dies, whatever the product's quality.

**4. What are you doing about the problem today?**

A workaround always exists: spreadsheets, manual processes, adjunct labor, patched-together tools. Name it, and estimate what it costs in time, money, or outcomes.

If nothing is being done and nobody minds, the problem is too mild to sustain a pilot.

### Part 2: Readiness assessment

Once you know what you need, check whether the institution can run a structured pilot.

| Question | Ready | Not yet |
|---|---|---|
| Do you have a named budget holder for edtech pilots? | A specific person can authorize pilot spending | "We'd need to find budget" |
| What is your procurement threshold before an RFP is required? | You know the dollar amount (for example, $50,000 or $100,000; see the [procurement guide](../procurement-guide.md#purchase-thresholds)) | You are not sure |
| Do you have a team that can run a 12-week pilot? | Instructional designers, IT support, and faculty champions are identified | "We'd need to assemble a team" |
| Can you provide a cohort of 200 or more students? | A specific course, program, or population is identified | "We'd need to find willing faculty" |
| Does your institution have a security review process (FERPA, SOC 2, [HECVAT](https://www.educause.edu/hecvat))? | The process exists and its timeline is known | No formal process, or the timeline is unknown |

**Scoring.** With 4 or 5 answers of "Ready," run a structured pilot. With 2 or 3, run a lightweight pilot, without full integration, to test demand before investing in the full process. With 0 or 1, return to needs discovery.

## Evaluation criteria

ScaleU uses this filter to judge whether an edtech company is ready for a structured institutional pilot. ScaleU designed it for seed-stage companies ($500,000 to $3M raised) with a working product. Institutions can adopt or adapt it. Founders can score themselves before approaching an institution.

### Hard filters (pass/fail)

Every company must pass five gates before scoring.

1. **An institutional buyer exists.** You can name a specific person at a specific institution who would champion the product: the dean of online learning at a named university, say. "Higher education" names no one.
2. **The problem is real.** Evidence shows that the problem exists and matters to institutional stakeholders. An assumption fails the gate.
3. **A product exists.** Users can use it today. A pitch deck or a prototype does not pass.
4. **Validation is possible.** A pilot can produce measurable evidence within the 12-18 month sales cycle. The pilot itself runs [12 weeks](#how-to-run-a-pilot-in-12-weeks); outcomes measured in a later term, such as retention, arrive after it ends. See [advising, retention, and persistence pilots](../pilot-benchmarks.md#advising-retention-and-persistence-pilots).
5. **The founder will engage.** The founder treats the pilot as a way to produce evidence, not as a sales demonstration.

A company that fails any gate is not ready for a structured pilot, whatever the product's quality.

### Eight-dimension scoring rubric

Score each dimension from 1 to 5. A company needs 24 of 40 to advance.

| Dimension | What it measures | Score 1 (weak) | Score 5 (strong) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Problem importance | Does this solve a problem institutions struggle with now? | Nice to have, no urgency | Critical gap, budget already allocated |
| Founder-market fit | Does the founder understand the institutional context? | New to education, learning on the job | Years of direct experience with the buyer |
| Founder quality | Can the founder execute with intellectual honesty? | Defensive about feedback, treats the pilot as sales | Treats the pilot as research, adapts to evidence |
| Solution effectiveness | Does the product work? | Demo stage, limited evidence | Deployed, with measurable outcomes and user retention data |
| Market scale | How large is the addressable market? | One institution, one use case | Many institutions and departments |
| Collaboration potential | Will the company be a partner? | Transactional, seeking a logo | Co-develops, responds to institutional needs |
| Ethical commitment | Does the product handle data, access, and equity responsibly? | No privacy policy, no accessibility plan | Signs the institution's data-privacy agreement, conforms to WCAG, publishes its data practices |
| Technical innovation | Is the technology defensible? | Commodity feature, easily replicated | Proprietary approach, durable advantage |

### The moat durability test

One more test for products built in the AI era: would this product survive a large language model and a two-week prompt-engineering sprint by a competent IT team?

Using AI earns no credit here. The test is whether the product's core value sits outside the model, where a sprint cannot reach it. Four moats pass. [Defensibility and moats](../../product/defensibility-moats.md) defines them in full, and its definitions govern:

1. **Proprietary data network.** Longitudinal data that improves with use and that a new entrant cannot reconstruct.
2. **Deep integration.** LTI and SIS write-back wired into the institution's daily work, so that switching costs more than the product does.
3. **Supply-side network effects.** Each added provider, mentor, employer, or contributor raises quality for every existing user. More students or more institutions on the buying side do not count.
4. **Regulated access.** Student-privacy, security, and accessibility approvals already granted by the institution: a signed data-privacy agreement, a completed security review, an accessibility review. No federal FERPA certification exists, and the paperwork alone is a head start that a funded competitor can match in months.

Being AI-powered is a feature layer, as exposed to substitution as any other software.

## How to run a pilot in 12 weeks

A structured pilot ends with evidence the institution can act on.

### Week 1-2: Design

1. **State the hypothesis.** Use the template: "[Target population] who use [product] will show [measurable outcome] compared to [control], as measured by [metric] over [time period]."
2. **Identify the cohort.** Aim for 200 or more students. This is a practitioner threshold: whether a cohort is large enough for statistical significance depends on the effect size you need to detect. For smaller, classroom-based pilots, see [pilot benchmarks](../pilot-benchmarks.md#participant-scale). Name the courses, the faculty champions, and the IT contact.
3. **Define success criteria.** Decide what outcome means go (proceed to procurement), what means pivot (adjust and test again), and what means no-go (end the relationship).
4. **Negotiate terms.** Agree on pilot cost, post-pilot pricing, data ownership, and support commitments before deployment, and record them in a [pilot agreement](../../templates/pilot-agreement.md). The [benchmarks page](../pilot-benchmarks.md#pilot-agreement-mou-elements) lists what the agreement should cover.

### Week 3-4: Setup

5. **Security review.** A data-privacy agreement under FERPA, the HECVAT, and a SOC 2 report, as the institution requires. Timelines stretch here, so start early. See [vendor review readiness](../vendor-review-readiness.md).
6. **Technical integration.** LMS integration (LTI 1.3), SSO, and data feeds; see [interoperability](../../product/interoperability.md). A lightweight pilot skips full integration and uses standalone access with manual enrollment.
7. **Faculty briefing.** The faculty champion must understand the hypothesis, their role, and what data will be collected.

### Week 5-10: Execution

8. **Launch with the cohort.** Track adoption (who uses it), engagement (how much), and the primary outcome metric.
9. **Meet the company weekly.** Review problems and what the data shows. Treat the company as a co-developer, not a vendor to be managed.
10. **Review at mid-pilot (week 7-8).** Check that actual adoption still allows a test of the hypothesis. If adoption is too low, find out why before the pilot ends.

### Week 11-12: Evidence

11. **Analyze results.** Test the primary outcome for statistical significance, and gather qualitative feedback from students and faculty. Whether a study is needed beyond the pilot depends on the evidence your buyers require; see [evidence tiers](../../evidence/evidence-tiers.md).
12. **Decision gate.** Go (the budget holder commits to procurement), pivot (adjust scope or population and test again), or no-go (end, with documented reasons).
13. **Debrief with the company.** Share the evidence, including negative findings. The debrief distinguishes a validation partner from a customer.

## What institutions care about

For founders, in order of influence on the decision (ScaleU's view).

**Deal-breakers**

- Passing security, privacy, and accessibility review (FERPA terms, SOC 2, WCAG)
- A named institutional champion who will own the pilot internally
- Pricing that works for a pilot, not enterprise pricing from the first day

**Differentiators**

- Evidence from prior deployments, even small ones
- Willingness to co-develop in response to pilot feedback
- Depth of integration with existing systems (Canvas, LMS workflows)

**Signals evaluators notice**

- The founder's understanding of the institutional context
- How the founder answers "What happens if the pilot doesn't work?"
- Whether the founder treats the pilot as research or as a sales opportunity

**What matters less than founders think**

- The technology itself. Institutions buy outcomes, not architecture.
- Awards and press coverage
- Being AI-powered, which every product now claims
