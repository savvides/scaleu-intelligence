# Buyer Demand Signals

**The question it answers:** what actually moves an institutional buyer off the status quo, and how do I tell real demand from polite interest? Use it to check a buyer's interest against patterns seen across other buyers, after you've done your own interviews ([jtbd-interviews.md](jtbd-interviews.md)).

The durable jobs institutional buyers switch for, and how to read real demand instead of polite interest. **Audience:** mixed. Most examples are K–12; others come from state CTE systems, employers, and higher ed. Each example names its setting.

*Source: ASU+GSV 2026 Summit Intelligence — ScaleU. CC BY 4.0. Practitioner signals, not peer-reviewed research.*

As of the ASU+GSV 2026 summit (April 2026), the patterns below came from analyzing switching behavior under the talking points of ten summit panels: not what speakers said they believe, but what they described doing differently. The full analysis is [Why Education Leaders Are Switching](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/deliverables/demand-signals.md). Jobs 1–3 come from its first round of five panels; those five switches are reconstructed in structured form, with [cross-panel patterns](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/jtbd/patterns-20260424.md), in the [JTBD switch data](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/jtbd/README.md). Jobs 4–6 and several signals come from a second round of five panels, analyzed from the transcripts.

**How to read the evidence:** these are conference panels, not structured interviews. Most narrators were vendors or institutional leaders describing their customers' switches, not their own. "From the summit" items are speaker claims, not independently verified results; each links to its transcript. The "Why they switch" paragraphs and the closing section are ScaleU's interpretation. Treat the patterns as directional. Validate with your own buyer conversations before betting on them.

The time-stamped specifics — the dated trend list and the named company roster from the summit — live in the [ASU+GSV 2026 conference collection](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/README.md). This file keeps only the durable patterns.

## The jobs buyers switch for

A job is not a feature or a product category. It's the thing a decision-maker is trying to get done. Buyers don't switch because your tool is better. They switch because the old model hit a wall they can't optimize past. Each job below names the buyer, why they move, and an example from the summit.

### 1. Reach more people without hiring proportionally more staff

**The job:** break a structural ceiling. Not "this could be better" but "this cannot grow without costs growing in lockstep."

**The buyer:** superintendents, provosts, state CTE directors, employer talent leads — anyone accountable for serving a population they physically cannot staff for.

**Why they switch:** the prior model had a hard cap. Either a physical limit (you can only run so many in-person sessions), institutional inertia (a mandate that never gets implemented across hundreds of sites), or a systemic one (an approach that produces real gains but grindingly slowly). The switch happens when someone finds a model that breaks the ceiling without proportional cost.

**From the summit:** SpringPod's CEO described an employer (Airbus) that capped its student work-experience program at about a hundred students a year because that was the in-person limit. Moving to virtual experiences, it now serves 10,000 a year — same employer engagement, 100x the reach ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/22-beyond-the-novelty-evaluating-ai-powered-career-navigation-tools.md)). Oklahoma's state CTE director, with 500-plus districts, watched a personalized-learning-plan mandate go unimplemented for years; schools walked students through plans on paper and checked the box. The fix wasn't a better platform. It was a field team where each person owns a geography, visits schools, and builds relationships with counselors. Technology plus people, not technology instead of people ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/25-turning-goals-into-scalable-systems-statewide-career-navigation-in-action.md)).

### 2. Stop wasting staff on mechanical tasks so they can do the human work

**The job:** return time and attention to the part of the role a person was actually hired for.

**The buyer:** principals, department chairs, CHROs, special-education directors — leaders watching skilled people burn hours on work that crowds out their core function.

**Why they switch:** the old approach wasn't failing because the technology was bad. It was failing because it required active effort from people who were already overloaded. The new model either removes the effort entirely or adds human support that makes the effort worth it.

**From the summit:** Walmart Academy's Josh Allen described a backroom inventory task that took about an hour; computer vision cut it to about twenty minutes ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/33-ai-is-the-new-key-to-unlocking-the-american-dream.md)). The point wasn't the time saved. It was giving the time back to customer-facing, human work. ClassDojo's CEO described replacing the "talk to a chatbot and tell it what to do" model with an ambient assistant that listens, helps, and acts so the teacher doesn't have to do anything different ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)). Kate Eberle Walker of Presence, a teletherapy vendor, said the average special-education clinician estimates about 40% of their time goes to administrative tasks instead of direct work with students, and that 40% is the target the company is selling against ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). On the same K–12 panel, a Cambium Learning Group executive named the data-silo version: teachers and administrators stuck acting as "human APIs" between systems that don't talk to each other. The job there isn't another tool. It's a layer that stops staff from being the integration glue.

### 3. Prove an outcome changed, not just engagement

**The job:** show that the tool moved an individual-level result, not a dashboard number.

**The buyer:** curriculum directors, provosts, board-accountable superintendents, L&D heads who have to defend a renewal.

**Why they switch:** prior approaches produced participation counts, logins, and completion rates that couldn't demonstrate anyone learned anything. That measurement vacuum made spend impossible to justify. The new model offers a specific, traceable, individual outcome.

**From the summit:** A career-navigation vendor (SpringPod) tracks where students land on LinkedIn after a program; it reported that of 30,000 students in one employer program, a third ended up in tech-aligned roles ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/22-beyond-the-novelty-evaluating-ai-powered-career-navigation-tools.md)). A destination metric, not a satisfaction survey. ClassDojo reported that kids using its K-5 reading tutor moved from pre-literacy to a second- or third-grade reading level in about five months at fifteen minutes a day ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)); this is a vendor's own figure, not an independent evaluation. Reed Hastings, speaking as an education philanthropist, pointed to chess.com, where he said today's typical ten-year-old scores far higher than ten-year-olds twenty years ago, as a sign that individualized practice with measured outcomes works at scale ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/09-class-disrupted-live-reed-hastings-on-the-ai-powered-future-of-learning.md)).

But the same vendor named the anxiety running under all of it: AI "will make it feel better and feel like magic, but actually, is it impacting that destination?" That fear haunts every switch. The worry isn't that the new tool fails. It's that it produces engagement without evidence, repeating the pattern that soured everyone on edtech in the first place.

### 4. Get my organization through the AI change curve before the technology laps us again

**The job:** absorb a moving capability frontier without the organization breaking. Distinct from job 2 — the goal isn't to save time, it's to not fall behind.

**The buyer:** superintendents, provosts, CHROs, district CTOs.

**Why they switch:** the bottleneck is no longer the technology. It's change management. Capability is sitting unused because institutions aren't ready to absorb it. Buyers who treat announcing a tool as deploying it get burned. A panelist with big-tech experience described two enterprise AI deployments of similar scope: one trained its people, the other said "start coding," and the second was "an abject failure," tens of millions of dollars spent for nothing. The panelist's takeaway: "it's not the technology" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/77-from-big-tech-to-edtech-harnessing-human-intelligence-in-the-ai-age.md)).

**From the summit:** At one session, the speaker (Alex Kotran of aiEDU, per the ScaleU analysis) asked the room who had enterprise access and training for agentic AI tools such as Claude Code; by the speaker's own estimate, "maybe 15% of the audience" did ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/82-beyond-the-pilot-ais-real-impact-on-learning-systems.md)). The room size wasn't recorded. (The 7,000 figure in some ScaleU summit notes is total summit attendance, not this session's audience.) The capability is there; institutional readiness is not. The buyer is no longer hiring an AI tool. They're hiring a way to get the organization through the change curve. This job fires one-off literacy workshops, vendor-led demos, and the assumption that a tool announcement counts as adoption.

### 5. Build the thing without becoming a developer

**The job:** ship something real without hiring engineers or learning to code. The institutional version: wrap a frontier-lab tool so it's safe for students.

**The buyer:** solo founders, in-house marketers and ops staff, teachers, and increasingly students themselves. For the institutional wrapper, K-12 districts and parent buyers.

**Why they switch:** the build-it-yourself option now exists at a price and skill level that didn't before. Lovable's CEO described building for "the 99%" of non-developers, including a nine-year-old whose online store took real orders ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/94-fireside-chat-with-lovable-and-imagi.md)). Once someone builds something themselves, their frame shifts: at one workshop, a speaker watched a student build a personal website in twenty minutes that had once taken weeks, and "their mind starts racing" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/82-beyond-the-pilot-ais-real-impact-on-learning-systems.md)). The frontier tools weren't built for kids, so a safety-and-pedagogy wrapper becomes its own product: the expertise is in making things age-appropriate, safe, and educational.

**From the summit:** This collapses the buyer/vendor line — your buyer can become a builder. For institutions, the derivative job is owning the wrapper layer that makes a general-purpose AI tool safe and pedagogically sound for students. Districts and parents reward whoever owns that seam. This job fires dev agencies, traditional no-code tools, and AP CS as the only on-ramp.

### 6. Turn a compliance mandate into a delivery system

**The job:** use the levers of compliance to make student support actually happen — and produce receipts that survive an audit.

**The buyer:** special-education directors, district CFOs, state DOE leaders.

**Why they switch:** compliance is structural budget. Wellness is discretionary budget. Special education is federally mandated, with required evaluation and service delivery; when compliance guardrails force the work to happen, that's how the most students get served. Mental health and wellness have no equivalent statutory backstop. In the words of Presence's Kate Eberle Walker, there's "so much lip service paid to it, so much talk about it, and so little funding" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/85-funding-the-future-of-student-support-special-needs-mental-health.md)). Vendors that productize the compliance receipt get adopted at the district line and renew. Vendors selling "student wellness" win the pilot, then lose the renewal when the line item gets cut.

**From the summit:** Presence, a teletherapy vendor, says it is winning business by showing "every minute of therapy, every IEP documented, how that attaches to the dollars spent" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). The job is both freeing clinician time and producing the audit-survivable artifact. This is the post-ESSER reality made concrete: per-minute logs, IEP audit trails, Medicaid billing automation. It fires awareness campaigns and "wellness" branding.

## Demand signals worth reading

These are durable patterns in how buyers behave, separate from any single year's trends. Read them as filters on whether the interest in front of you is real.

### Trust is built through receipts and relationships, not UI

The most vivid moment across the panels, from Oklahoma CTE director Brent Haken ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/25-turning-goals-into-scalable-systems-statewide-career-navigation-in-action.md)): adoption works "when they have somebody that they already trust and they can really rely on to have their cell phone number and call them and cry over the phone when they can't figure it out." That's not a UX problem. It's a relationship problem. Products that rely on product-led growth inside institutions are likely underinvesting in the human layer that makes adoption stick. Trust also gets built through compliance receipts and through giving people a builder identity. Different vehicles, same need: institutions don't adopt what they don't feel ownership of. Ask yourself who the trusted-human equivalent is for your product, and whether your business model can afford one.

### One wrong AI answer triggers full abandonment

Frontline users who get a wrong answer from an AI tool often don't iterate. They quit the tool entirely. As Walmart Academy's Josh Allen put it: "you get a wrong answer, and I mean, a lot of people are just going to discount it... sometimes people are like, I give up" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/33-ai-is-the-new-key-to-unlocking-the-american-dream.md)). Deterministic or visual outputs (a green-check confirmation, a reading score) carry lower abandonment risk than probabilistic text generation. Where text generation is necessary, visible confidence signals and easy correction loops matter more than your accuracy percentage. This applies directly to any AI tool put in front of teachers or students.

### Post-ESSER buyers want dollar-attributed outcomes

The K–12 procurement reality, as Presence's Kate Eberle Walker described it: "we are in a value for money era in K-12. This is not the ESSER era... I got to be able to show as a school administrator that I've been thoughtful in spending every dollar" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). Outcome data alone no longer closes the gap. The buyer isn't asking "did this work?" They're asking "can I show my board exactly what my dollars bought?" Build the receipt layer — usage tied to spend, individual outcomes attached to line items — before you build another analytics dashboard.

### Institutions suppress their own tools' best features

Reed Hastings recalled from his involvement with DreamBox, an early adaptive-math product, that districts asked the company to turn off the adaptive pacing because students who moved ahead became "disruptive and bored" in class ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/09-class-disrupted-live-reed-hastings-on-the-ai-powered-future-of-learning.md)). The institution bought the differentiator and then disabled it. This isn't a sales problem you can close around. It's a structural constraint of how classrooms work. If your product has adaptive or personalized features, test whether buyers will suppress them after deployment — feature-adoption audits at 30, 60, and 90 days catch it. Suppression is a leading indicator of churn, not a config preference.

### Friction is a signal you're reading the buyer right

ClassDojo designs its K-5 reading tutor around fifteen minutes a day ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)). OMA Play, a screenless AI device in development for ages three to five, has no face, takes naps, shuts off at night, and by design won't turn on or do what the child wants about 40% of the time ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/84-living-learning-with-aliens-the-complex-psychology-of-ai-anthropomorphism.md)). Anthropic said its consumer Claude.ai product is 18-plus ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/48-every-student-should-have-an-ai-learning-companion.md)). These are responses to real parent and pediatric-psychiatry anxiety about screen time and emotional dependency in brain-formation windows. Common Sense Media's 2025 survey of US teens aged 13–17 found that 72% had used AI companions and 52% used them regularly ([Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions), July 2025; cited on stage by Common Sense's Ellen Pack). The question is no longer whether kids engage but whether anyone shapes how. In ScaleU's reading, products optimizing for daily-active-use as a north-star metric in K-12 will collide with this hard. Building friction in early reads a real signal. It isn't playing it safe.

### Staff fear job loss before they adopt

"The first thing people get when they get AI is, am I going to lose my job?" (ParentSquare's Sohit Wadhwa, [transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). The staff you're asking to adopt the tool are the same staff who suspect it's there to replace them. Eberle Walker said the "no screens in classrooms" backlash, taken to its extreme, is a challenge for teletherapy, and the same logic reaches AI-companion products ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/85-funding-the-future-of-student-support-special-needs-mental-health.md)). Anxiety about outcomes, dependency, and jobs is the force that quietly kills switches — and success-story panels systematically underrepresent it. Assume it's stronger than what you hear.

## What this means for reading your own demand

*ScaleU's interpretation of the patterns above.*

The push that moves a buyer is a structural ceiling, not incremental dissatisfaction. The pull that lands the switch is technology combined with a human relationship layer, not technology alone. The anxiety that almost stops the switch is the fear of engagement without outcomes. The habit that keeps buyers stuck is deploying a platform and expecting behavior change to follow.

So when you read demand: a buyer naming a wall they can't optimize past is real signal. A buyer who likes your demo but can't name what it would replace or what outcome it would prove is not. Match the signals above against the buyer in front of you before you scale toward them.
