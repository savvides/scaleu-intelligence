# Buyer demand signals

Institutional buyers switch when the old model hits a limit they cannot optimize past, and they stay switched when the new one comes with people they trust and proof they can show a board. This page lists six jobs buyers described switching for at ASU+GSV 2026 and six signals for telling demand from polite interest. Use it to check one buyer's interest against patterns across many, after your own [Switch interviews](jtbd-interviews.md).

**Applies to:** mostly K-12; other examples come from state career and technical education (CTE) systems, employers, and higher ed. Each example names its setting. United States.

*Source: ASU+GSV 2026 Summit Intelligence, ScaleU. CC BY 4.0. Practitioner signals, not peer-reviewed research.*

## How to read the evidence

The patterns come from ten panels at the ASU+GSV summit in April 2026, analyzed for what speakers described doing differently and not for what they said they believe. Three cautions apply.

- **These are conference panels, not structured interviews.** Most narrators were vendors or institutional leaders describing their customers' switches, not their own.
- **Examples attributed to speakers are speaker claims.** None was independently verified. Each links to its transcript. The transcripts are machine-made and do not label who is speaking, so attributions within a panel rest on context.
- **The interpretation is ScaleU's.** The "Why they switch" paragraphs, the readings under each signal, and the closing section are judgment. Treat the patterns as directional and test them in your own buyer conversations.

The full analysis is [Why Education Leaders Are Switching](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/deliverables/demand-signals.md). Jobs 1–3 surfaced in a first round of five panels; those five switches are reconstructed in structured form, with [cross-panel patterns](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/jtbd/patterns-20260424.md), in the [JTBD switch data](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/jtbd/README.md). A second round of five panels, analyzed from the transcripts, added examples to jobs 1–3 and surfaced jobs 4–6 and several of the signals. The dated trend list and the roster of companies named at the summit are in the [ASU+GSV 2026 conference collection](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/README.md); this page keeps the durable patterns.

## The six jobs at a glance

A job is what a decision-maker is trying to get done. It is neither a feature nor a product category.

| Job | Typical buyers | The limit that forces the switch |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Reach more people without hiring proportionally more staff | Superintendents, provosts, state CTE directors, employer talent leads | A model that cannot grow unless costs grow with it |
| 2. Stop wasting staff on mechanical tasks | Principals, department chairs, chief human resources officers (CHROs), special-education directors | Skilled people losing hours to work that crowds out their role |
| 3. Prove an outcome changed, not just engagement | Curriculum directors, provosts, superintendents, L&D heads | Spending that logins and completion rates cannot justify |
| 4. Get the organization through the AI change curve | Superintendents, provosts, CHROs, district CTOs | Capability sitting unused because the institution cannot absorb it |
| 5. Build the thing without becoming a developer | Solo founders, operations staff, teachers, students; districts and parents for the safe wrapper | Building now costs little in money or skill |
| 6. Turn a compliance mandate into a delivery system | Special-education directors, district CFOs, state education leaders | Mandated services that must be delivered and documented |

## The jobs buyers switch for

### 1. Reach more people without hiring proportionally more staff

**The job:** break a structural ceiling. The buyer's complaint is that the program cannot grow unless costs grow in step.

**The buyer:** superintendents, provosts, state CTE directors, and employer talent leads; anyone accountable for a population they cannot staff for.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** the prior model had a hard cap. The cap may be physical (only so many in-person sessions), institutional (a mandate never implemented across hundreds of sites), or systemic (an approach that works but slowly). The switch comes when someone finds a model that breaks the ceiling without proportional cost.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *Employer.* SpringPod's CEO described an employer, Airbus, that capped its student work-experience program at about 100 students a year, the in-person limit. With virtual experiences it now serves 10,000 a year ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/22-beyond-the-novelty-evaluating-ai-powered-career-navigation-tools.md)).
- *State career navigation.* A Vermont panelist said that state law has mandated personalized learning plans since 2013, and that few principals would claim to do them in a meaningful way. Oklahoma's state CTE director, Brent Haken, whose state has more than 500 districts, said his schools had been checking the box too. What changed it, he said, was a field team: each member owns a region, visits every school, and helps counselors use the platform. He called it the biggest change in a decade and said technology does nothing without people who build trust ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/25-turning-goals-into-scalable-systems-statewide-career-navigation-in-action.md)).

### 2. Stop wasting staff on mechanical tasks so they can do the human work

**The job:** return time and attention to the work a person was hired for.

**The buyer:** principals, department chairs, CHROs, and special-education directors; leaders who watch skilled people spend hours on work that crowds out their main function.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** the old approach failed because it demanded effort from people already overloaded, and the technology itself was seldom the fault. The new model either removes the effort or adds human support that makes the effort worthwhile.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *Employer.* Walmart Academy's Josh Allen described a backroom inventory task that took about an hour; computer vision cut it to about 20 minutes. In ScaleU's reading, the value lay in returning that time to customer-facing work ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/33-ai-is-the-new-key-to-unlocking-the-american-dream.md)).
- *K-12.* ClassDojo's CEO described replacing the model in which a teacher instructs a chatbot with an ambient assistant that listens, helps, and acts, so the teacher does nothing differently ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)).
- *K-12 special education.* Kate Eberle Walker of Presence, a teletherapy vendor, said the average special-education clinician estimates that about 40% of their time goes to administrative tasks and not to direct work with students. That 40% is what the company sells against ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)).
- *K-12 data.* On the same panel, a Cambium Learning Group executive described teachers and administrators acting as "human APIs" between systems that do not exchange data. The job there is a layer that relieves staff of doing the integration by hand, not another tool.

### 3. Prove an outcome changed, not just engagement

**The job:** show that the tool moved a result for individual learners, not a dashboard number.

**The buyer:** curriculum directors, provosts, board-accountable superintendents, and L&D heads who must defend a renewal.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** earlier approaches produced participation counts, logins, and completion rates that could not show anyone learned anything, so the spending could not be justified. The new model offers a specific, traceable outcome for each individual.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *Career navigation.* SpringPod tracks where students land on LinkedIn after a program. It reported that of 30,000 students in its Amazon program, a third ended up in tech-aligned roles: a destination metric, not a satisfaction survey ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/22-beyond-the-novelty-evaluating-ai-powered-career-navigation-tools.md)).
- *K-12.* ClassDojo reported that children using its K-5 reading tutor moved from pre-literacy to a second- or third-grade reading level in about five months at 15 minutes a day ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)). This is the vendor's own figure, not an independent evaluation.
- *Consumer.* Reed Hastings, speaking as an education philanthropist, pointed to chess.com, where he said today's typical ten-year-old scores far higher than ten-year-olds did twenty years ago, as a sign that individualized practice with measured outcomes works at scale ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/09-class-disrupted-live-reed-hastings-on-the-ai-powered-future-of-learning.md)).

The same career-navigation vendor named the anxiety beneath all of this: AI can make a product "feel like magic" without changing where students end up. Buyers fear less that a new tool will fail than that it will produce engagement without evidence, which is the pattern that soured many of them on edtech.

### 4. Get my organization through the AI change curve before the technology laps us again

**The job:** absorb a moving capability frontier without breaking the organization. This differs from job 2: the aim is to keep up, not to save time.

**The buyer:** superintendents, provosts, CHROs, and district CTOs.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** the bottleneck has moved from the technology to change management. Capability sits unused because institutions are not ready to absorb it, and buyers who treat an announcement as a deployment get burned. The buyer is hiring a way through the change, not an AI tool. This job displaces one-off literacy workshops, vendor-led demos, and the assumption that announcing a tool counts as adopting it.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *Enterprise.* A panelist described two telecommunications companies that set out to automate customer service with AI. One spent heavily on educating its staff and succeeded. The other told the vendor to start coding and was "an abject failure," with tens of millions of dollars spent for nothing. Another panelist told of an insurance tool that worked and was canceled, and blamed change management, not the technology ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/77-from-big-tech-to-edtech-harnessing-human-intelligence-in-the-ai-age.md)).
- *Summit audience.* At one session the speaker (Alex Kotran of aiEDU, per the ScaleU analysis) asked who had enterprise access to, and at least one training on, Claude Code or Claude Cowork. By the speaker's own estimate, "maybe 15% of the audience" did ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/82-beyond-the-pilot-ais-real-impact-on-learning-systems.md)). The size of the room was not recorded. The 7,000 figure in some ScaleU summit notes is total summit attendance, not this session's audience.

### 5. Build the thing without becoming a developer

**The job:** ship something real without hiring engineers or learning to code. The institutional version is to wrap a frontier-lab tool so that it is safe for students.

**The buyer:** solo founders, in-house marketers and operations staff, teachers, and increasingly students. For the institutional wrapper, K-12 districts and parents.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** building it yourself is now possible at a price and skill level that did not exist before, so a buyer can become a builder. The frontier tools were not built for children, so a wrapper that makes them age-appropriate, safe, and educational becomes a product in its own right, and districts and parents reward whoever owns that layer. This job displaces development agencies, traditional no-code tools, and AP Computer Science as the only on-ramp.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *Consumer.* Lovable's CEO described building for "the 99%" who are not developers, and said he had bought an item from a store that a nine-year-old built with the product ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/94-fireside-chat-with-lovable-and-imagi.md)).
- *Students.* At one workshop, a speaker watched a student build in 20 minutes a personal website that had once taken weeks, and said that students who build something themselves start generating ideas of their own ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/82-beyond-the-pilot-ais-real-impact-on-learning-systems.md)).

### 6. Turn a compliance mandate into a delivery system

**The job:** use compliance requirements to make student support happen, and produce records that survive an audit.

**The buyer:** special-education directors, district CFOs, and state department of education leaders.

**Why they switch (ScaleU's interpretation):** compliance is structural budget; wellness is discretionary budget. Special education is federally mandated, with required evaluation and service delivery. Mental health and wellness have no equivalent statutory backing. Vendors that make the compliance record their product get adopted at the district line and renew. Vendors selling student wellness win the pilot and lose the renewal when the line item is cut. After the federal ESSER relief funds, this means per-minute logs, IEP audit trails, and automated Medicaid billing, and it displaces awareness campaigns and wellness branding.

**From the summit (speaker claims):**

- *K-12 special education.* Presence, the teletherapy vendor, says it wins business by documenting every minute of therapy and every IEP and tying each to the dollars spent ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). The product both frees clinician time and produces the record an audit requires.
- *K-12 mental health.* Eberle Walker said that the most students get served where compliance forces the work to happen, and that far less happens in mental health and wellness, which lack those levers. Her interviewer, John Rogers of TPG's Rise Fund, added that mental health draws much talk and "so little funding" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/85-funding-the-future-of-student-support-special-needs-mental-health.md)).

## Demand signals worth reading

These patterns in buyer behavior outlast any one year's trends. Use them to judge whether the interest in front of you is real. The speaker's statement in each is a speaker claim; the reading that follows is ScaleU's.

### Receipts and relationships build trust; interfaces do not

Oklahoma CTE director Brent Haken said adoption works when school staff have someone they already trust, whose cell number they have, and whom they can call and "cry over the phone" with when they are stuck ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/25-turning-goals-into-scalable-systems-statewide-career-navigation-in-action.md)). That is a relationship problem, and no interface solves it. Products that count on product-led growth inside institutions probably underinvest in the human layer that makes adoption last. Compliance records build trust too, and so does letting people build something themselves; institutions adopt what they feel they own. Ask who the trusted human is for your product, and whether your business model can pay for one.

### One wrong AI answer triggers full abandonment

Frontline users who get a wrong answer from an AI tool often quit it without trying again. Walmart Academy's Josh Allen said that after one wrong answer many people discount the tool, and some say "I give up" ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/33-ai-is-the-new-key-to-unlocking-the-american-dream.md)). Deterministic or visual outputs, such as a green check or a reading score, carry less risk of abandonment than generated text. Where you must generate text, visible confidence signals and easy correction matter more than your accuracy percentage. This applies to any AI tool put before teachers or students.

### Post-ESSER buyers want dollar-attributed outcomes

Presence's Kate Eberle Walker described K-12 purchasing after ESSER as an era of value for money, in which an administrator must show care in spending every dollar ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). Outcome data alone no longer closes a sale. The buyer needs to show a board what the dollars bought. Build the receipt layer first: usage tied to spending, and individual outcomes attached to line items. The analytics dashboard can wait.

### Institutions suppress their own tools' best features

Reed Hastings, an early backer of the adaptive-math product DreamBox, recalled that districts asked the company to turn off adaptive pacing because students who got ahead became "disruptive and bored" in class ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/09-class-disrupted-live-reed-hastings-on-the-ai-powered-future-of-learning.md)). The institution bought the differentiator and then disabled it. No sales technique closes around this; it is a constraint of how classrooms work. If your product adapts or personalizes, audit feature adoption at 30, 60, and 90 days to learn whether buyers have switched those features off. Suppression is a leading indicator of churn.

### Friction is a signal you're reading the buyer right

Several products at the summit limit their own use by design:

- ClassDojo designs its K-5 reading tutor around 15 minutes a day ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/37-building-with-the-cool-kids-the-new-architecture-of-classroom-engagement.md)).
- OMA Play, a screenless AI device in development for ages three to five, has no face, takes naps, shuts off at night, and by design declines to turn on or do what the child wants about 40% of the time ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/84-living-learning-with-aliens-the-complex-psychology-of-ai-anthropomorphism.md)).
- Anthropic said its consumer Claude.ai product is for users 18 and older ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/48-every-student-should-have-an-ai-learning-companion.md)).

These choices answer parents' and pediatric psychiatrists' anxiety about screen time and emotional dependency while young brains are forming. Common Sense Media's 2025 survey of US teens aged 13–17 found that 72% had used AI companions and 52% used them regularly ([Talk, Trust, and Trade-Offs](https://www.commonsensemedia.org/research/talk-trust-and-trade-offs-how-and-why-teens-use-ai-companions), July 2025; cited on stage by Common Sense's Ellen Pack). Children already engage; the open question is whether anyone shapes how. In ScaleU's reading, K-12 products that optimize for daily active use will collide with this anxiety, and designing friction in early responds to a real signal.

### Staff fear job loss before they adopt

A panelist who, by context, was ParentSquare's Sohit Wadhwa, describing the rollout of AI to his own company's staff, said the first thing people ask when they get AI is whether they will lose their job ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/52-the-operating-system-of-the-modern-school.md)). Expect the same of school staff: the people you ask to adopt the tool suspect it is there to replace them. Eberle Walker said the backlash against screens in classrooms, taken to its extreme, is a challenge for teletherapy ([transcript](../../sources/events/2026-asu-gsv/transcripts/85-funding-the-future-of-student-support-special-needs-mental-health.md)). In ScaleU's reading, the same backlash reaches AI-companion products. Anxiety about outcomes, dependency, and jobs is the force that quietly ends switches, and panels of success stories underrepresent it. Assume it is stronger than what you hear.

## What this means for reading your own demand

*ScaleU's interpretation of the patterns above, in the terms of the [four forces](jtbd-interviews.md#the-four-forces).*

| Force | What it looked like across the panels |
|---|---|
| Push | A structural ceiling, not incremental dissatisfaction |
| Pull | Technology combined with a human relationship layer |
| Anxiety | Fear of engagement without outcomes |
| Habit | Deploying a platform and expecting behavior to change |

A buyer who names a wall they cannot optimize past is giving you a real signal. A buyer who likes your demo and cannot name what it would replace, or what outcome it would prove, is not. Check the buyer in front of you against these signals before you scale toward them.
