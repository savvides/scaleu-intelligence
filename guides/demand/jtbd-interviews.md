# JTBD Switch interviews for edtech

To learn whether anyone will switch to your product and pay for it, interview people who have already switched tools in your category, and reconstruct what they did. Ask about past behavior, never about future intentions. Use this method when you keep hearing "this sounds great" and cannot tell whether it means demand.

**Applies to:** K-12 districts, universities, and corporate learning and development (L&D). The examples mix all three and name the role. United States.

*Adapted from the [JTBD Switch toolkit](https://github.com/savvides/jtbd) (MIT), itself based on Bob Moesta's Jobs-to-be-Done Switch method. The edtech examples and the advice on institutional buyers are ScaleU's practitioner view.*

## The method in brief

| Step | What you do |
|---|---|
| 1. Recruit | Find people who recently switched, churned, or are evaluating a tool in your category. Include the budget holder and an approver, not only users. |
| 2. Reconstruct | Start at the decision and walk backward along the timeline to the first thought. |
| 3. Probe | Ask directly about each of the four forces: push, pull, anxiety, habit. |
| 4. Redirect | When you hear speculation, steer back to what happened. |
| 5. Compare | After three or more interviews, look for the push, the anxiety, and the habit that repeat. |

The premise is that people hire a product to make progress. A registrar had a routine before your tool appeared: transcripts in a shared drive, a wait-list spreadsheet, a part-time evaluator. The routine was poor, and it got the job done. Then something broke, the registrar looked around, chose one option, and switched. Reconstruct that story and you learn what to build and how to sell it. Ask the same registrar whether a concept sounds useful and you learn only that they are polite.

## Where the method comes from

Clayton Christensen's jobs theory holds that customers hire products to do a job. He and his coauthors set it out in the Harvard Business Review article [Know Your Customers' "Jobs to Be Done"](https://hbr.org/2016/09/know-your-customers-jobs-to-be-done) (Christensen, Hall, Dillon, and Duncan, September 2016) and in the book [*Competing Against Luck*](https://www.christenseninstitute.org/books/competing-against-luck/) (2016).

The interview technique comes from practitioners. Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek developed the [four-forces model](https://jobstobedone.org/the-four-forces/) as part of their Jobs-to-be-Done interviewing method, and they teach the [Switch interview](https://jobstobedone.org/switch-interview/) as a backward walk through a real purchase. Moesta, whom Christensen mentored for many years, applied the method to selling in *Demand-Side Sales 101* (with Greg Engle, 2020; discussed in this [Intercom interview](https://www.intercom.com/blog/podcasts/bob-moesta-on-unleashing-your-sales-superpower), October 2020).

The job story format came from Intercom's product team in 2013; Alan Klement later named it ([Paul Adams, Intercom, 2016](https://www.intercom.com/blog/accidentally-invented-job-stories/)).

## The four forces

Four forces act on every switch. Two drive the buyer to change and two hold the buyer back. The switch happens only when push and pull together outweigh anxiety and habit.

| Force | Direction | The buyer's question |
|---|---|---|
| Push of the current situation | Drives the switch | What is broken now? |
| Pull of the new solution | Drives the switch | What is attracting me? |
| Anxiety about the new solution | Resists the switch | What if it doesn't work? |
| Habit of the present | Resists the switch | What am I used to? |

Score each force you find from 1 to 10: 10 for the dominant driver of the decision, 1 for a force that barely mattered. The number is directional. A 3 against an 8 means something; a 6 against a 7 means nothing. You are ranking what moved the buyer.

The quotations below illustrate how each force sounds. Treat them as illustrations, not as evidence.

### Push: what is breaking now

Push is what is failing in the status quo. It is tied to a specific event or pattern. "It was frustrating" is not a push. "The transcript backlog ran past the enrollment deadline and we lost the student" is.

- A provost: "Our 7.5-week courses had a 30% drop rate and the board started asking why."
- A transfer student: "My GPA dropped a full point the term I didn't have an advisor who could tell me what to take next."
- A district CTO: "The old gradebook went down during the last week of the term and teachers couldn't post finals."
- An L&D lead: "Completion on our compliance training sat at 40% and audit season was coming."
- A registrar: "Manual credit evaluation took three weeks and students were committing to the competitor that answered first."

### Pull: what the new solution promises

Pull is the appeal of an imagined future, and buyers describe it as a situation, seldom as a feature. Nobody is drawn to "real-time articulation." A registrar is drawn to telling an admitted student their credit status before the deadline.

- A registrar: "I pictured students seeing exactly which credits would count the day they applied, not a month later."
- A provost: "I wanted to see the drop-risk numbers myself, without asking institutional research to pull a report."
- An L&D lead: "I imagined onboarding a new hire to job-ready in half the time, and being able to show that number to my VP."
- A faculty member teaching 200 online students: "I pictured giving every student real feedback without working until midnight."

When you hear a pull, ask why it matters until you reach the personal payoff: credibility, no longer being the bottleneck, standing well with leadership. That payoff is the pull.

### Anxiety: what scares them about switching

Anxiety is whatever makes the buyer hesitate. Buyers seldom volunteer it, so ask: "What almost stopped you?"

- A registrar: "What if we lose three years of transcript data in the migration?"
- A district CTO: "What if this thing touches FERPA records and we end up with a breach during the pilot?"
- A provost: "What if faculty hate it and I've spent political capital pushing a tool nobody uses?"
- An L&D lead: "What if procurement classifies it high-risk and it sits in security review for six months?"
- A dean: "What if the vendor turns out worse than what we already have, and now I own that decision?"

### Habit: the comfort of the familiar

Habit is the hold of the present arrangement. People tolerate great pain to avoid changing how they work, and they build workarounds so familiar that they forget they are workarounds. Interviewers probe this force least.

- A registrar: "Every evaluator had their own way of dealing with the old system's quirks. Retraining them felt brutal."
- An advising office: "Our whole intake process was built around the degree-audit tool nobody can read. Someone's entire job was translating it into plain English for students."
- A district: "Teachers had years of materials inside the old LMS. Moving felt like starting over."
- An L&D team: "We had reports the CFO expected in a specific format. Rebuilding them was its own project."

A described workaround ("my analyst had a whole process for that") reveals two forces at once: habit, because the routine is comfortable, and push, because the routine exists only to compensate for a broken tool. Probe both.

## Job stories

A user story ("As a user, I want X so that Y") assumes a persona and omits the situation that triggers the need. A job story puts the situation first:

**When [situation], I want [motivation], so I can [outcome].**

The situation is the moment the job appears. The motivation is what the person reaches for. The outcome is the progress they seek. Two examples drawn from the [higher ed jobs atlas](higher-ed-jobs-atlas.md):

> **When** a transfer student applies and hasn't seen their credit articulation, **I want** to show them exactly which credits count before the enrollment deadline, **so I can** stop losing them to the competitor that answered first.

> **When** I'm teaching 200 online students and can't give any of them real feedback, **I want** to draft high-quality feedback at scale that I review and send, **so I can** stop students feeling ignored without working until midnight.

"As an instructor, I want better feedback tools" lets you skip the moment, the stakes, and the alternative. The job story requires all three, so it tells you when to appear and what you replace.

## The backward-timeline interview

Every switch passes through the same stages. Reconstruct them from the end, because people remember a decision and can retrace the path that led to it.

1. **First thought.** The moment they realized things could be different, usually a specific event such as a gradebook failing during finals.
2. **Passive looking.** Casual awareness. They ask peers, read comparison threads, and notice that tools exist. They are not yet searching.
3. **Active looking.** Something raised the urgency, and now they book demos and run trials. Find out what changed.
4. **Deciding.** The moment they chose. The deciding factor is seldom the feature matrix; often it is emotional or circumstantial, such as a dean who sat in on the demo and said to buy it.
5. **Consuming.** Onboarding, migration, adoption. Here anxiety becomes real, as when the team learns it must re-enter a term of data by hand.

Moesta and Spiek's own version of the timeline marks an event between passive and active looking and another before the decision. Those events matter most.

### How to walk the timeline

Open with: "Tell me about the last time you switched from one [category] to another." If they have not switched, ask about the last time they seriously considered it. Then walk backward.

1. **Decision.** "What did you end up choosing? When was that? Who else was in the room?"
2. **Active looking.** "Before that, what else did you evaluate? How did you find them? What moved you from annoyed to actively searching?"
3. **Passive looking.** "Before you were searching, did you know alternatives existed? How long did you know there was something better and do nothing?"
4. **First thought.** "Take me back to the first moment you thought you might look for something different. What happened?"
5. **Consuming.** "After you decided, what was onboarding like? What was harder than expected?"

The event that turned passive looking into active looking is the most valuable thing you can learn. It is usually specific: a missed deadline, a bad board meeting, an embarrassment in front of a superintendent. Probe until you have the story. Then listen: a common rule of thumb is to talk no more than about 20% of the time.

### Four interview contexts

| Interviewee | What you learn | Adjust the interview |
|---|---|---|
| Switched to you | Why your wedge works and what almost stopped them | The default. Reconstruct the full timeline and all four forces. |
| Switched away from you | Your failures, as their push | Treat your product as the old way. Add "What would have kept you?" and "When did you first think about leaving?" |
| Switched between competitors | The forces in your category, undistorted by your product | Ask what made them look, what they evaluated, and why you were not on the list. |
| Still evaluating | The anxiety or habit blocking them now | Skip the decision stage. Add "What would need to be true for you to make a change?" and "What's stopping you right now?" |

The churn interview is the most painful and the most valuable. "Too expensive" is almost never the real reason for leaving, since a buyer who paid had already accepted the price. Dig past it.

### Probe each force directly

The timeline surfaces some forces unprompted. Ask about the rest.

- **Push:** "What was the most frustrating thing about the old way? Tell me about a specific time it let you down. What did that cost you?"
- **Pull:** "What first caught your attention? When you imagined using it, what did you picture being different?"
- **Anxiety:** "What almost stopped you? Was there a moment you nearly backed out?" Let the silence sit. If they say "nothing," try "If you had to name one risk, even a small one?"
- **Habit:** "Was there anything about the old way you'd miss? Did anyone on your team resist? What workarounds did you have to give up?"

## Red flags

Each of these means you are hearing speculation in place of memory. Redirect at once.

| Red flag | Sounds like | Redirect |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothetical language | "I would…", "I think I'd…" | "I want what actually happened. Take me back to that moment." |
| Vague emotion | "It was frustrating." | "Tell me about a specific time. What happened?" |
| Category answer | "We needed better analytics." | "What specifically couldn't you do? Walk me through the last time it was a problem." |
| No dates or people | "At some point we decided…" | "Was that before or after [event]? Who was in the room?" |
| Feature list | "It has dashboards and an API and…" | "Which mattered most to you? Why that one?" |
| Pleasing you | "Your product is great because…" | "I appreciate that, but I'm after the messy middle. What almost stopped you?" |

Hypothetical language is the most serious. "I would definitely use a tool that does X" is a wish. A count of people already running a workaround is far stronger evidence than any number of people who say they would use something. Switch interviews earn their trust by asking what someone did.

## Interviewing institutions

No single person decides an institutional purchase, and each approver has their own four forces. Interview the budget holder and at least one approver, such as IT or procurement, as well as the user (see [Who else must say yes?](demand-validation.md#5-who-else-must-say-yes)). Eight surveyed teachers are noise. A registrar who already runs a manual workaround is signal.

After three or more interviews, patterns appear: the same push event, the same anxiety blocking the same buyers, the same habit to break. The repeated pattern, and no single story, is the job your product serves.
