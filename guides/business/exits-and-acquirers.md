# Exits and acquirers: who buys edtech companies

Most edtech companies that exit are bought by a private-equity-owned platform or a large publisher, in small deals at low revenue multiples. Public offerings are rare, and half of the 2021 class has since left the market. Decide early which buyer you are building for, and keep the records that buyer will inspect.

**Applies to:** K-12, higher ed, workforce, and consumer; United States, with one Indian case. Deal terms come from company releases, SEC filings, and trade press, each linked. Ownership changes often; check a company's current owner before you act on this page.

## What the record shows

- **Many deals, mostly small.** HolonIQ counted about 410 edtech merger and acquisition transactions worldwide in 2025, 22 of them private equity acquisitions ([HolonIQ, February 6, 2026](https://www.holoniq.com/notes/edtech-hits-2-6b-in-investment-as-the-market-stabilizes-bigger-bets-in-ai-and-workforce-training)). Berkery Noyes, counting the wider education industry, tracked 244 transactions in the first half of 2026: 181 by strategic buyers and 63 by private equity ([Berkery Noyes](https://berkerynoyes.com/1st-half-2026-education-industry-trends/)).
- **Low prices unless you are large.** In the same Berkery Noyes data, the median disclosed price was 1.3 times revenue in the first half of 2026, down from 2.3 in the prior half. Over 30 months, deals between $10M and $160M had a median of 1.7 times revenue; deals above $160M, 5.9 times. Only 136 of the 999 deals in that sample disclosed terms, and the sample includes childcare and training services.
- **Strategic buyers prefer small additions.** Tyton Partners' review of 2025 found private equity activity in education down about 25% and strategic acquisitions up 4% globally, mostly as tuck-ins. One investor it quoted said "six years feels like the new three" for holding periods ([Tyton Partners, January 13, 2026](https://tytonpartners.com/2025-education-sector-deal-recap-a-year-of-reset-and-disruption/)).

## Four cautionary cases

- **Byju's: growth financed by debt.** Lenders of a term loan of about $1.16B alleged that $533M had been moved out of their reach, and won a Delaware court order giving them control of the US unit, Byju's Alpha. India's company tribunal admitted the parent, Think & Learn, into insolvency on July 16, 2024 ([Outlook Business](https://www.outlookbusiness.com/corporate/byjus-to-seek-out-of-court-settlement-with-bcci-after-nclt-admits-insolvency-petition-report)). On November 20, 2025, a Delaware bankruptcy court entered a default judgment of more than $1B against founder Byju Raveendran, whose adviser said he would appeal ([The Tribune](https://www.tribuneindia.com/news/business/us-court-orders-byju-to-pay-over-usd-1-billion-to-byjus-alpha-glas-trust)). The litigation continues; check its status.
- **2U: debt and a contested model.** The online program manager and owner of edX filed a prepackaged Chapter 11 on July 25, 2024, to cut debt by more than half to about $459M ([2U](https://2u.com/newsroom/strengthening-2us-financial-position-for-sustained-innovation-and-growth/)), and emerged on September 13, 2024, as a private company ([2U](https://2u.com/newsroom/2u-successfully-completes-transaction/)).
- **Anthology: a roll-up that came apart.** Anthology filed for Chapter 11 in 2025, sold its administrative and student-success units to Ellucian and Encoura, and emerged on March 2, 2026, as a debt-free Blackboard ([Blackboard](https://www.blackboard.com/news/blackboard-formerly-anthology-emerges-debt-free-and-focused)). Campus Technology reported an analyst's expectation that investors Nexus and Oaktree would control it ([October 2, 2025](https://campustechnology.com/Articles/2025/10/02/Anthology-Restructures-Focuses-on-Teaching-and-Learning-Business.aspx)).
- **Pluralsight: a price the business could not carry.** Vista took it private in 2021 at about $3.5B. In August 2024, according to press reports summarized by the law firm Goodwin, Vista handed ownership to its lenders in a deal that cut about $1.3B of debt ([Goodwin](https://goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2024/08/alerts-privateequity-ma-pluralsight-change-of-control-transaction)).

The common cause was borrowing against growth that stopped. In all four, creditors and courts, not customers, decided who owned the company.

## What acquirers value

This is a practitioner view, consistent with the deals above.

| What they look for | What they inspect |
|---|---|
| Recurring revenue | Multi-year or auto-renewing contracts paid from recurring budgets; services and one-time grant purchases counted separately ([unit economics](unit-economics.md)) |
| Retention | Renewal by customer cohort, and usage that explains it |
| Distribution they lack | A subject, grade band, state adoption, or buyer relationship the acquirer has not won |
| Data and integrations | Rights to use the data you hold, and production integrations with the systems the acquirer sells |
| Compliance posture | Signed privacy agreements, security reports, accessibility reports, and a clean incident history. Renaissance bought Illuminate two months after New York City's schools stopped using its products over a breach. Renaissance said the purchase was unrelated to the breach and did not disclose the price. A breach need not end a company, but it costs customers |

## What this means for your strategy

**Choose the likely buyer and build toward its gap.** The four types want different things (ScaleU's view):

1. **A private-equity-owned platform** (Instructure, PowerSchool, Renaissance, Discovery Education) buys products it can sell to customers it already has. Integrate with that platform, win shared customers, and make cross-selling arithmetic easy.
2. **A publisher or curriculum company** (McGraw Hill, Pearson, Curriculum Associates, IXL) buys subjects and capabilities missing from its catalog. In 2026 that has included AI tools for teachers.
3. **A private equity firm buying directly** wants profit, retention, and a sales process that runs without the founder. Growth alone will not do.
4. **A workforce software company** (Workday, Coursera) buys products that enterprises already pay for. Workday's $1.1B for Sana is the largest disclosed price in the table of strategic deals.

A partner's change of owner is also a risk to you: roadmaps and partner terms move when Watermark, Keypath, or Instructure changes hands.

**Keep these clean from the first customer.**

- Intellectual property: signed invention assignments from every founder, employee, and contractor, and a record of open-source licenses.
- Data rights: contracts that say what you may do with customer data, including whether you may train models on it and what happens on a change of control.
- Contract terms: assignment clauses that permit a sale without each customer's consent, and a single folder of every signed agreement.
- Revenue records: recurring, services, and pilot revenue reported separately, by customer and year ([business models and pricing](business-models-and-pricing.md)).
- Compliance history: dated privacy agreements, security and accessibility reports, and an incident log.
- Capital structure: a current cap table, few classes of stock, and no debt you could not repay from a flat year.

The same files form the investor data room in [fundraising](fundraising.md).
