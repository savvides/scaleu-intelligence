# Fundraising for edtech founders

Raise venture capital only if your company can plausibly reach the size at which edtech companies sell for high multiples; most cannot, and a grant-funded or profitable path often leaves the founder with more. If you do raise, expect six standard objections and answer each with your own data.

**Applies to:** K-12, higher ed, workforce, and consumer; United States. For who the investors, grant programs, and accelerators are, see the [funding landscape](../market/funding-landscape.md). This page covers how to decide and how to prepare.

## Decide whether to raise venture capital

A venture investor needs a few companies in each fund to return the whole fund. Test your plan against what buyers pay. Berkery Noyes, an investment bank that tracks education deals, reported a median price of 1.3 times revenue for education transactions with disclosed terms in the first half of 2026, down from 2.3 in the prior half. Across its 30-month sample the median was 1.7 times revenue for deals between $10M and $160M and 5.9 times for deals above $160M ([Berkery Noyes, first half 2026](https://berkerynoyes.com/1st-half-2026-education-industry-trends/)). Its sample covers the whole education industry, including childcare and training services, and only the minority of deals that disclose terms, so treat the figures as a floor for software, not a forecast.

The arithmetic matters. At 1.7 times revenue, a company with $10M of revenue sells for $17M, and a seed fund holding 10% receives $1.7M. That outcome can change a founder's life and still fail the fund. Raise venture money if you can show a path past roughly $160M in enterprise value, where the multiples change (ScaleU's view, drawn from the Berkery Noyes split above).

| Path | Fits when | What you give up | Verified example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venture-backed for-profit | The market supports a company worth hundreds of millions, and speed decides who wins | Control, and the option of a modest sale: preferred stock gets paid first | Duolingo raised at least $183M privately before its July 2021 IPO ([Crunchbase News, July 30, 2021](https://news.crunchbase.com/edtech/edtech-2021-ipos/)) |
| Profitable niche business (grants, revenue, angels) | The segment is real but small: one subject, one state, one role | Speed, and the ability to outspend a funded rival | None cited; private companies of this kind rarely publish figures |
| Public benefit corporation (PBC) | You want venture capital and a mission written into the charter | Little. Directors must balance stockholders, people affected by the company, and the stated benefit ([8 Del. C. §§ 362, 365](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/sc15/index.html)) | Coursera converted to a Delaware PBC and earned B Corp certification before its 2021 IPO ([Coursera, February 2, 2021](https://blog.coursera.org/coursera-receives-b-corp-certification)) |
| Nonprofit | Your users cannot pay and philanthropy will; free access is the strategy | Equity. No investors, no stock options, no sale proceeds | Khan Academy is a [501(c)(3) nonprofit](https://www.khanacademy.org/about) |

Two points on structure. B Corp is a private certification from B Lab; PBC is a legal form. They are separate, and a company can hold either or both. Legal form also sets grant eligibility: SBIR is for for-profit small businesses, while a nonprofit can apply directly for Education Innovation and Research grants, which a company can join only as a partner (see [Grants](../market/funding-landscape.md)).

## The six objections and how to answer them

Every edtech investor has seen these fail. Answer with numbers from your own business; a market report persuades no one. The objections are a practitioner view; the linked facts are sourced.

| Objection | What is true | The answer that works |
|---|---|---|
| The market is small | Many edtech categories are bounded by a countable set of institutions | A bottom-up count: buyers of your type, times your actual contract value, from public data ([market data sources](../market/market-data-sources.md)), plus the second product those buyers have asked for |
| Sales cycles are long | Institutional purchases follow budget calendars and procurement thresholds ([procurement guide](../selling/procurement-guide.md)) | Your measured cycle, from first meeting to signature, by deal size, and the purchases you kept under the bid threshold |
| Buyers pay slowly | Districts and universities pay on purchase orders and net terms | Annual contracts billed up front, days to collect, and a cash forecast built on collections, not bookings ([unit economics](unit-economics.md)) |
| Large exits are rare | Typical deals price near 1 to 2 times revenue (above). Large ones exist: Instructure sold for about $4.8B and PowerSchool for about $5.6B in 2024 ([exits and acquirers](exits-and-acquirers.md)) | Name three likely acquirers, what each has bought, and why your revenue is the kind they pay for |
| Policy can erase demand | ESSER relief ended, and SBIR authority lapsed for six months in 2025–2026 ([funding landscape](../market/funding-landscape.md)) | Revenue split by the customer's funding source, with the share on recurring budget lines |
| AI will commoditize it | Chegg cut about 45% of its staff in October 2025, citing generative AI and lost search traffic ([Fortune, October 28, 2025](https://fortune.com/2025/10/28/chegg-layoffs-ceo-dan-rosensweig-poster-child-ai-slashing-staff-shares/)) | What a general-purpose model cannot supply: your data, integrations, or regulated access ([defensibility moats](../product/defensibility-moats.md)) |

The climate makes these objections sharper. HolonIQ counted $2.6B of global edtech venture funding in 2025 and noted that funding had not returned to its 2020–2021 highs ([HolonIQ, February 6, 2026](https://www.holoniq.com/notes/edtech-hits-2-6b-in-investment-as-the-market-stabilizes-bigger-bets-in-ai-and-workforce-training)).

## What investors check at each stage

This table is a practitioner view, not sourced market data. Round sizes and revenue expectations by stage are in the [funding landscape](../market/funding-landscape.md).

| Stage | The question | What they check |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-seed | Does this founder know the buyer? | Interview notes and reference calls with real buyers; who pays, from which budget line; a logic model for why the product should work ([evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md), Tier 4) |
| Seed | Do users return, and will anyone pay? | Usage by cohort; paid pilots and their conversion to contracts ([pilot benchmarks](../selling/pilot-benchmarks.md)); first signed privacy agreements; a pricing model that survives procurement ([business models and pricing](business-models-and-pricing.md)) |
| Series A | Is the sale repeatable? | Renewal and expansion by cohort; sales cycle and acquisition cost by channel ([unit economics](unit-economics.md)); customer references the investor picks; correlational or better evidence of outcomes (Tier 3); a defensible answer on AI ([defensibility moats](../product/defensibility-moats.md)) |

Investors call customers. Tell your references the call is coming, and never list one who would hesitate to renew.

## Sequence grants with equity

Non-dilutive money buys research and evidence. It rarely buys sales. Use it in this order (ScaleU's view):

1. **Before equity, apply for SBIR Phase I** if your product needs research and development that a customer would not fund. Award sizes and solicitation rules are in the [funding landscape](../market/funding-landscape.md). Start early: the application-to-award gap can outlast a pre-seed runway, and the 2025–2026 lapse showed the program can stop.
2. **Raise the pre-seed or seed on customer traction**, and present the grant as research that the investors do not have to pay for. Keep grant-funded work and commercial work in separate budgets; federal awards carry reporting and audit duties.
3. **Use Phase II or foundation money to fund the efficacy study** that institutional buyers and Series A investors ask for ([evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md)).
4. **Keep grants a minority of income.** Investors read heavy grant dependence as proof that customers will not pay.

Foundations mostly fund nonprofits and research partners. A for-profit usually reaches that money as a subcontractor or partner on someone else's grant.

## Instruments in brief

- **SAFE.** A right to future stock, with no interest or maturity date. Y Combinator publishes the standard post-money forms: a valuation cap, a discount, or an uncapped most-favored-nation version, plus a user guide with conversion math ([YC documents](https://www.ycombinator.com/documents)). On a post-money SAFE the investor's ownership is the amount invested divided by the cap, so $1M on a $10M cap sells 10%. Add up every SAFE before signing the next one; founders who stack them learn the total dilution only at the priced round.
- **Priced round.** The company sells preferred stock at a set price, with a board seat and protective provisions. The National Venture Capital Association publishes free model documents, including the certificate of incorporation, stock purchase agreement, investors' rights agreement, and voting agreement ([NVCA model legal documents](https://nvca.org/model-legal-documents/)).

Neither set of forms replaces a lawyer who closes venture financings every month.

## Data room checklist for edtech

Beyond the standard corporate, financial, and cap-table folders, an edtech investor expects these. Preparing them also prepares you for buyers' vendor reviews ([vendor review readiness](../selling/vendor-review-readiness.md)).

- **Privacy:** signed student data privacy agreements, by state or institution; your privacy policy and subprocessor list; how you handle deletion requests ([K-12 regulatory guide](../market/k12-regulatory.md)).
- **Security:** a completed security questionnaire of the kind your buyers send, your most recent penetration test or audit report, and your incident history, including none.
- **Accessibility:** a current Accessibility Conformance Report (the completed VPAT) and the date of the last audit.
- **Efficacy:** every study, with design, sample, and who ran it, labeled by ESSA tier ([evidence tiers](../evidence/evidence-tiers.md)). Include null results; investors find them anyway.
- **Pilots:** each pilot with start date, price, success criteria, and outcome, and the pilot-to-paid conversion rate computed from that list.
- **Renewals:** revenue by customer cohort and year, showing renewal, expansion, and churn, with the reason for each loss.
- **Contracts:** term, renewal date, payment terms, termination-for-convenience and non-appropriation clauses, and any cooperative purchasing contracts you sell through.
- **Customer funding sources:** the share of revenue paid from one-time grants versus recurring budgets.
- **Integrations and AI:** rostering, single sign-on, and LMS integrations in production ([interoperability](../product/interoperability.md)); which AI models you use, what student data they receive, and your vendor terms ([AI risk and trust](../product/ai-risk-and-trust.md)).

## Common mistakes

All practitioner view.

- **Raising venture capital for a niche business.** The preference stack then blocks the modest sale that would have paid the founder well.
- **Counting pilots as revenue.** Free pilots are costs. Report paid pilots and conversions separately from recurring contracts.
- **Presenting engagement as outcomes.** Usage shows that people log in. It does not show that anyone learned.
- **Timing the raise against the school year.** If the round closes after spring budget decisions, the money arrives too late to sell into the next year.
- **Hiding concentration.** One district or one university system that supplies most of the revenue is the first thing diligence finds.
- **Leaving paperwork for later.** A missing privacy agreement or accessibility report delays both the round and the next contract.
